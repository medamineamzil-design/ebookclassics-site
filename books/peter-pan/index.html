About the book

Peter Pan tells the story of the Darling children, Wendy, John, and Michael, in Edwardian London, whose nursery is visited one night by Peter Pan, a boy who never grows up, searching for his shadow, which Wendy sews back onto him. Peter, accompanied by the fiercely jealous fairy Tinker Bell, invites the children to fly with him, using fairy dust and happy thoughts, to Neverland, an island of perpetual childhood adventure populated by the Lost Boys, who fell out of their prams and were never claimed; hostile pirates led by the menacing Captain Hook, who lost his hand to a crocodile in a duel with Peter and now lives in terror of the same crocodile, which swallowed a ticking clock and now announces its approach; and a native tribe led by the princess Tiger Lily. In Neverland Wendy quickly settles into a maternal role for the Lost Boys, telling them stories and mending their clothes, while the children have a series of episodic adventures, rescuing Tiger Lily from the pirates, an encounter with a mermaid lagoon, and repeated skirmishes with Hook's crew, all narrated with Peter as the fearless, forgetful, and often startlingly callous hero at the center. Hook, obsessed with defeating Peter and reclaiming his dignity, eventually captures the Lost Boys and Wendy, forcing them toward his ship, and poisons Peter's medicine, a plot foiled when Tink drinks the poison herself to save him, nearly dying before Peter's belief, and that of children everywhere clapping to affirm they believe in fairies, revives her. Peter storms Hook's ship alone, defeats the pirates and finally Hook himself, who leaps into the jaws of the waiting crocodile. Wendy, having grown homesick and increasingly aware that Neverland's timeless adventure cannot substitute for real family, decides to return to London with her brothers and the Lost Boys, who are adopted by the Darling parents. Peter, incapable of accepting ordinary domestic life or growing up, returns alone to Neverland, though the book's closing pages reveal he continues to visit Wendy each spring for her to do his "spring cleaning," until, years later, an adult Wendy watches her own daughter fly off with Peter in her place, the cycle of childhood renewing itself endlessly without him.

About J. M. Barrie

Scottish novelist and playwright (1860-1937), best known for creating the character Peter Pan.

How it begins

All children, except one, grow up. They soon know that they will grow up, and the way Wendy knew was this. One day when she was two years old she was playing in a garden, and she plucked another flower and ran with it to her mother. I suppose she must have looked rather delightful, for Mrs. Darling put her hand to her heart and cried, “Oh, why can’t you remain like this for ever!” This was all that passed between them on the subject, but henceforth Wendy knew that she must grow up. You always know after you are two. Two is the beginning of the end.

Of course they lived at 14, and until Wendy came her mother was the chief one. She was a lovely lady, with a romantic mind and such a sweet mocking mouth. Her romantic mind was like the tiny boxes, one within the other, that come from the puzzling East, however many you discover there is always one more; and her sweet mocking mouth had one kiss on it that Wendy could never get, though there it was, perfectly conspicuous in the right-hand corner.
