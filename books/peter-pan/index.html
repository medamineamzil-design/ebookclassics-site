About the book

In a London nursery, Wendy, John and Michael Darling are looked after by their devoted parents and by Nana, a dog who serves as their nurse. One night a boy named Peter Pan, who refuses to grow up, flies in through the window with the fairy Tinker Bell. He teaches the children to fly and takes them away to the Neverland, an island of mermaids, fairies and the Lost Boys, where Wendy is invited to be a mother to Peter's band. Their great enemy is the pirate Captain Hook, who is himself pursued by a crocodile that once swallowed a clock.

The story is full of adventure and comedy, but Barrie's narrator also writes with a wry, bittersweet awareness of childhood, memory, and the cost of growing up. Peter is charming, brave and forgetful, both enviable and a little sad. Modern readers will notice attitudes of its time, especially in the depiction of the island's native people, but the book's imaginative energy and its reflections on parents and children remain striking.

Peter Pan first appeared in Barrie's 1902 novel The Little White Bird and then in his hugely successful stage play of 1904. Barrie turned the play into this novel, published in 1911 as Peter and Wendy. In 1929 he gave the rights to Great Ormond Street Hospital for Children in London. The story has been adapted countless times on stage and screen.

Peter Pan tells the story of the Darling children, Wendy, John, and Michael, in Edwardian London, whose nursery is visited one night by Peter Pan, a boy who never grows up, searching for his shadow, which Wendy sews back onto him. Peter, accompanied by the fiercely jealous fairy Tinker Bell, invites the children to fly with him, using fairy dust and happy thoughts, to Neverland, an island of perpetual childhood adventure populated by the Lost Boys, who fell out of their prams and were never claimed; hostile pirates led by the menacing Captain Hook, who lost his hand to a crocodile in a duel with Peter and now lives in terror of the same crocodile, which swallowed a ticking clock and now announces its approach; and a native tribe led by the princess Tiger Lily. In Neverland Wendy quickly settles into a maternal role for the Lost Boys, telling them stories and mending their clothes, while the children have a series of episodic adventures, rescuing Tiger Lily from the pirates, an encounter with a mermaid lagoon, and repeated skirmishes with Hook's crew, all narrated with Peter as the fearless, forgetful, and often startlingly callous hero at the center. Hook, obsessed with defeating Peter and reclaiming his dignity, eventually captures the Lost Boys and Wendy, forcing them toward his ship, and poisons Peter's medicine, a plot foiled when Tink drinks the poison herself to save him, nearly dying before Peter's belief, and that of children everywhere clapping to affirm they believe in fairies, revives her. Peter storms Hook's ship alone, defeats the pirates and finally Hook himself, who leaps into the jaws of the waiting crocodile. Wendy, having grown homesick and increasingly aware that Neverland's timeless adventure cannot substitute for real family, decides to return to London with her brothers and the Lost Boys, who are adopted by the Darling parents. Peter, incapable of accepting ordinary domestic life or growing up, returns alone to Neverland, though the book's closing pages reveal he continues to visit Wendy each spring for her to do his "spring cleaning," until, years later, an adult Wendy watches her own daughter fly off with Peter in her place, the cycle of childhood renewing itself endlessly without him.

About J. M. Barrie

James Matthew Barrie was born in 1860 in Kirriemuir, Scotland, the son of a weaver. When he was six, his older brother David died in a skating accident, and his mother's grief marked him deeply. He studied at Edinburgh University and worked as a journalist before moving to London. He found success with tales of Scottish village life and stage comedies. His friendship with the Llewelyn Davies family inspired Peter Pan, and he later became guardian of their five sons. He died in London in 1937.

Peter Pan first appeared in The Little White Bird in 1902; the play opened in London in 1904 and later became the novel Peter and Wendy. The story of the boy who takes Wendy and her brothers to Neverland, with fairies, pirates and Captain Hook, became a lasting classic. His other plays include The Admirable Crichton and Mary Rose. In 1929 he gave the rights to Peter Pan to Great Ormond Street children's hospital.

How it begins

All children, except one, grow up. They soon know that they will grow up, and the way Wendy knew was this. One day when she was two years old she was playing in a garden, and she plucked another flower and ran with it to her mother. I suppose she must have looked rather delightful, for Mrs. Darling put her hand to her heart and cried, “Oh, why can’t you remain like this for ever!” This was all that passed between them on the subject, but henceforth Wendy knew that she must grow up. You always know after you are two. Two is the beginning of the end.

Of course they lived at 14, and until Wendy came her mother was the chief one. She was a lovely lady, with a romantic mind and such a sweet mocking mouth. Her romantic mind was like the tiny boxes, one within the other, that come from the puzzling East, however many you discover there is always one more; and her sweet mocking mouth had one kiss on it that Wendy could never get, though there it was, perfectly conspicuous in the right-hand corner.

The way Mr. Darling won her was this: the many gentlemen who had been boys when she was a girl discovered simultaneously that they loved her, and they all ran to her house to propose to her except Mr. Darling, who took a cab and nipped in first, and so he got her. He got all of her, except the innermost box and the kiss. He never knew about the box, and in time he gave up trying for the kiss. Wendy thought Napoleon could have got it, but I can picture him trying, and then going off in a passion, slamming the door.

Mr. Darling used to boast to Wendy that her mother not only loved him but respected him. He was one of those deep ones who know about stocks and shares. Of course no one really knows, but he quite seemed to know, and he often said stocks were up and shares were down in a way that would have made any woman respect him.

Mrs. Darling was married in white, and at first she kept the books perfectly, almost gleefully, as if it were a game, not so much as a Brussels sprout was missing; but by and by whole cauliflowers dropped out, and instead of them there were pictures of babies without faces. She drew them when she should have been totting up. They were Mrs. Darling’s guesses.

Wendy came first, then John, then Michael.

For a week or two after Wendy came it was doubtful whether they would be able to keep her, as she was another mouth to feed. Mr. Darling was frightfully proud of her, but he was very honourable, and he sat on the edge of Mrs. Darling’s bed, holding her hand and calculating expenses, while she looked at him imploringly. She wanted to risk it, come what might, but that was not his way; his way was with a pencil and a piece of paper, and if she confused him with suggestions he had to begin at the beginning again.
