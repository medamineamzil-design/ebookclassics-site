About the book

Set among the landed gentry of early nineteenth-century rural England, Pride and Prejudice follows Elizabeth Bennet, the sharp-witted second of five daughters in a family whose modest estate is entailed away from them upon their father's death, leaving their mother, Mrs. Bennet, single-mindedly obsessed with marrying her girls off before that day arrives. The plot is set in motion when the wealthy, amiable Mr. Bingley leases the nearby estate of Netherfield and quickly falls for Elizabeth's gentle elder sister Jane, while Bingley's even wealthier and far haughtier friend, Mr. Darcy, attends a local assembly and is overheard by Elizabeth dismissing her as merely "tolerable" — an insult that hardens into an instant, deeply held prejudice she carries through most of the novel. Their paths cross repeatedly over the following months: through Jane's blossoming romance with Bingley, which is abruptly and mysteriously interrupted; through the arrival of the charming militia officer George Wickham, whose account of Darcy's supposed cruelty toward him confirms every negative impression Elizabeth already holds; through the ridiculous, socially anxious clergyman Mr. Collins, who proposes to Elizabeth and, when refused, promptly marries her practical best friend Charlotte Lucas instead; and through Darcy's own unexpected and remarkably clumsy first proposal of marriage, delivered with as much condescension as affection, which Elizabeth furiously and comprehensively rejects. Stung, Darcy writes Elizabeth a long letter explaining the true, far less flattering circumstances behind both Wickham's grievances and his own interference in Jane and Bingley's romance, and this letter begins to unravel Elizabeth's certainty in her own judgment. A chance meeting at Darcy's grand estate of Pemberley, where Elizabeth witnesses firsthand his quiet generosity toward his tenants and his sister, further complicates her feelings, and when Elizabeth's youngest sister Lydia scandalously elopes with Wickham, it is Darcy who secretly intervenes to salvage the family's reputation. Only once Elizabeth has fully reassessed both her own pride in her judgment and her prejudice against Darcy's character does the novel resolve its long chain of misunderstandings, moving toward two marriages — Jane's and Elizabeth's — built, unlike so many others in the book, on genuine mutual respect.

How it begins

“Why, my dear, you must know, Mrs. Long says that Netherfield is taken by a young man of large fortune from the north of England; that he came down on Monday in a chaise and four to see the place, and was so much delighted with it that he agreed with Mr. Morris immediately; that he is to take possession before Michaelmas, and some of his servants are to be in the house by the end of next week.”

“What is his name?”

“Bingley.”

“Is he married or single?”

“Oh, single, my dear, to be sure! A single man of large fortune; four or five thousand a year. What a fine thing for our girls!”
