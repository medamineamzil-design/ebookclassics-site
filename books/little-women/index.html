About the book

Little Women follows the four March sisters as they grow up in a small New England town while their father is away serving with the Union army in the American Civil War. Sensible Meg longs for pretty things, tomboyish Jo dreams of becoming a writer, gentle Beth loves music and home, and youngest Amy has artistic ambitions and a taste for elegance. Guided by their wise mother, whom they call Marmee, the girls face money worries, quarrels and small adventures, and befriend Laurie, the lively boy who lives next door with his grandfather.

The novel is warm, funny and honest about the ordinary struggles of growing up: vanity, temper, shyness, envy and the difficulty of choosing one's own path. Jo, impatient with the expectations placed on girls, remains one of literature's most beloved heroines. Alcott writes about family affection, work, poverty and women's independence with a directness that still feels fresh, and many readers find in the sisters a reflection of their own families.

Alcott drew on her own childhood with her three sisters in Massachusetts. The first part was published in 1868 and was so successful that a second part followed in 1869; in Britain it was long issued separately as Good Wives. Alcott continued the family's story in Little Men and Jo's Boys. The book has been adapted many times for film, television, stage and opera.

Little Women follows the four March sisters, Meg, Jo, Beth, and Amy, growing up in Concord, Massachusetts during and after the Civil War, with their father away serving as a chaplain and their mother, Marmee, holding the household together on modest means. The novel opens on a lean Christmas and quickly establishes each sister's temperament: Meg is gentle and a little vain about the finer things she cannot afford; Jo is headstrong, bookish, and impatient with the constraints placed on girls, dreaming of becoming a writer; Beth is shy, musical, and almost saintly in her selflessness; Amy is artistic, image-conscious, and prone to childish vanity that slowly matures into genuine grace. Their world expands through friendship with the wealthy boy next door, Laurie, who becomes a near-brother to them all and, for a long stretch of the novel, is presumed to be Jo's eventual match. The first half follows small domestic dramas, a burned manuscript, a disastrous skating accident, a matchmaking scheme, a trip to a fashionable but morally hollow social set, that teach the sisters lessons about vanity, temper, and generosity. The second half, often published separately as Good Wives, follows the sisters into adulthood: Meg marries the tutor John Brooke and adjusts to genteel poverty; Jo moves to New York, pursues writing, and eventually finds an unexpected match in the older, intellectual Professor Bhaer rather than Laurie, who proposes to her and is turned down; Beth's fragile health, weakened years earlier by scarlet fever contracted while helping a poor family, gradually fails, and her death is one of the novel's most enduring set pieces; Amy travels to Europe, refines her art and manners, and ultimately marries Laurie herself. The novel closes with the surviving sisters settled into domestic and professional lives, gathered again at Marmee's side, the family bond intact despite loss and change.

About Louisa May Alcott

Louisa May Alcott was born in 1832 in Germantown, Pennsylvania, the second of four daughters of the Transcendentalist educator Amos Bronson Alcott. She grew up mostly in Concord, Massachusetts, where Emerson and Thoreau were family friends. Because her father rarely earned money, she worked as a seamstress, governess, teacher and servant and wrote to support her family. During the Civil War she served as a nurse in Washington until she fell seriously ill. A supporter of abolition and women's rights, she died in Boston in 1888, two days after her father.

Alcott's Hospital Sketches drew on her nursing experience, and she also wrote sensational thrillers under a pen name. Little Women, published in 1868 and 1869, follows the four March sisters growing up during the Civil War, with the independent Jo inspired by Alcott herself. Its warmth, humour and honest picture of family life made it a lasting success. She continued the story in Little Men and Jo's Boys.

How it begins

Jo was the first to wake in the gray dawn of Christmas morning. No stockings hung at the fireplace, and for a moment she felt as much disappointed as she did long ago, when her little sock fell down because it was crammed so full of goodies. Then she remembered her mother’s promise and, slipping her hand under her pillow, drew out a little crimson-covered book. She knew it very well, for it was that beautiful old story of the best life ever lived, and Jo felt that it was a true guidebook for any pilgrim going on a long journey. She woke Meg with a “Merry Christmas,” and bade her see what was under her pillow. A green-covered book appeared, with the same picture inside, and a few words written by their mother, which made their one present very precious in their eyes. Presently Beth and Amy woke to rummage and find their little books also, one dove-colored, the other blue, and all sat looking at and talking about them, while the east grew rosy with the coming day.

In spite of her small vanities, Margaret had a sweet and pious nature, which unconsciously influenced her sisters, especially Jo, who loved her very tenderly, and obeyed her because her advice was so gently given.

“Girls,” said Meg seriously, looking from the tumbled head beside her to the two little night-capped ones in the room beyond, “Mother wants us to read and love and mind these books, and we must begin at once. We used to be faithful about it, but since Father went away and all this war trouble unsettled us, we have neglected many things. You can do as you please, but I shall keep my book on the table here and read a little every morning as soon as I wake, for I know it will do me good and help me through the day.”

Then she opened her new book and began to read. Jo put her arm round her and, leaning cheek to cheek, read also, with the quiet expression so seldom seen on her restless face.

“How good Meg is! Come, Amy, let’s do as they do. I’ll help you with the hard words, and they’ll explain things if we don’t understand,” whispered Beth, very much impressed by the pretty books and her sisters’ example.

“I’m glad mine is blue,” said Amy. and then the rooms were very still while the pages were softly turned, and the winter sunshine crept in to touch the bright heads and serious faces with a Christmas greeting.

“Where is Mother?” asked Meg, as she and Jo ran down to thank her for their gifts, half an hour later.

“Goodness only knows. Some poor creeter came a-beggin’, and your ma went straight off to see what was needed. There never was such a woman for givin’ away vittles and drink, clothes and firin’,” replied Hannah, who had lived with the family since Meg was born, and was considered by them all more as a friend than a servant.
