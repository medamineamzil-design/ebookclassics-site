About the book

Billy Budd, a handsome and good-natured young sailor, is pressed into service in 1797 from the merchant ship Rights-of-Man onto a British man-of-war, at a time when the Royal Navy has just been shaken by the mutinies at Spithead and the Nore. Admired by the crew, he arouses the envy of the master-at-arms, John Claggart, who falsely accuses him before Captain Edward Fairfax Vere of plotting mutiny. Unable to answer because of his stammer, Billy strikes his accuser.

Melville's novella is a tragedy of innocence and evil, law and conscience. The volume also contains other prose pieces by Melville, including the essay "Hawthorne and His Mosses" and stories such as "Cock-a-Doodle-Doo!" and "The Paradise of Bachelors and the Tartarus of Maids".

Billy Budd was left unfinished at Melville's death in 1891 and first published in 1924 in this edition by Raymond Weaver, as volume XIII of the Standard Edition of Melville's works. Later editions corrected Weaver's text; in this version the warship is called the Indomitable, renamed Bellipotent in later editions.

About Herman Melville

Herman Melville was born in New York City in 1819. His father's business failed, and he died when Herman was twelve. After working as a clerk and teacher, Melville went to sea, eventually on a whaler in the Pacific. He deserted in the Marquesas Islands and returned home on a navy frigate. He married Elizabeth Shaw and befriended Nathaniel Hawthorne. Later he worked for nineteen years as a customs inspector in New York, where he died in 1891, largely forgotten.

Melville's first books, Typee and Omoo, drew on his South Sea adventures and were popular. Moby-Dick, published in 1851, tells of Captain Ahab's obsessive hunt for a white whale, mixing adventure, philosophy and whaling lore; it sold poorly. He went on to write stories such as Bartleby, the Scrivener, the novel The Confidence-Man, and Billy Budd, published only in 1924. A revival in the 1920s established Moby-Dick as one of the great American novels.

How it begins

‘Billy Budd,’ the title-piece of this volume, is a novel finished by Melville five months before his death in 1891, and never before published. ‘Daniel Orme’ is a sketch ‘omitted from Billy Budd.’ The fourth piece--‘The Two Temples’--was on May 12, 1854, refused by Putnam’s Monthly Magazine out of a fear of offending the religious sensibilities of the congregation of Grace Church, New York. This volume concludes with eight sketches surviving in manuscript, written, in all probability, after Melville’s retirement in 1886, at the age of sixty-seven, from his post as Inspector of Customs in New York City. Except for his letters, journals, and the juvenile ‘Fragments from a Writing Desk,’ this closes the count of Melville as a writer of prose. The rest of the volume comprises all Melville’s contributions to magazines that he acknowledged but never reprinted.

The text of matter hitherto unpublished has, so far as possible, been printed verbatim from Melville’s manuscript. Here and there, however, owing to the heavily corrected condition of many of the papers, slight adjustments in the interests of grammar or of style have been made in Melville’s wording.

The editor and publishers are indebted to the Princeton University Press for their courtesy in allowing two of the essays included in this volume to be reprinted from The Apple-Tree Table and Other Sketches, a volume of Melville’s prose miscellanea recently issued from Princeton.

Preface

The year 1797, the year of this narrative, belongs to a period which, as every thinker now feels, involved a crisis for Christendom, not exceeded in its undetermined momentousness at the time by any other era whereof there is record. The opening proposition made by the Spirit of that Age,[1] involved a rectification of the Old World’s hereditary wrongs. In France, to some extent, this was bloodily effected. But what then? Straightway the Revolution itself became a wrongdoer, one more oppressive than the kings. Under Napoleon it enthroned upstart kings, and initiated that prolonged agony of continual war whose final throe was Waterloo. During those years not the wisest could have foreseen that the outcome of all would be what to some thinkers apparently it has since turned out to be, a political advance along nearly the whole line for Europeans.

Now, as elsewhere hinted, it was something caught from the Revolutionary Spirit that at Spithead emboldened the man-of-war’s men to rise against real abuses, long-standing ones, and afterwards at the Nore to make inordinate and aggressive demands, successful resistance to which was confirmed only when the ringleaders were hung for an admonitory spectacle to the anchored fleet. Yet in a way analogous to the operation of the Revolution at large, the Great Mutiny, though by Englishmen naturally deemed monstrous at the time, doubtless gave the first latent prompting to most important reforms in the British Navy.
