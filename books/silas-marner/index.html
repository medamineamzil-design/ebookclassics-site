About the book

Silas Marner is a weaver and a devout member of a small chapel congregation at Lantern Yard, in a town in the north of England. Falsely accused of theft, betrayed by his closest friend and abandoned by his fiancée, he loses his faith and moves to the village of Raveloe. There he lives alone for fifteen years, working at his loom and finding comfort only in the gold coins he earns. Meanwhile, at the Red House, the squire's sons Godfrey and Dunstan Cass have secrets of their own.

Eliot tells a story close to a fable, about loss, trust and the way love and community can bring a broken life back into the world. She paints village life with warmth and humour, catching the talk of its inhabitants at the local inn, while showing how chance, conscience and choice shape people's fates.

Silas Marner: The Weaver of Raveloe, Eliot's third novel, was published on 2 April 1861 by William Blackwood and Sons. Short and accessible, it became one of her most widely read works and has long been studied in schools. The Spanish translation offered here, by an unnamed translator, was published in Buenos Aires in 1919 by the Biblioteca de La Nación.

About George Eliot

Mary Ann Evans was born in 1819 on an estate in Warwickshire, where her father worked as land agent. A brilliant student, she lost her evangelical faith as a young woman, causing a painful rift with her father. She translated German theological works and moved to London, where she became assistant editor of the Westminster Review. She lived openly for over twenty years with the married writer George Henry Lewes, which scandalised society. She married John Cross in 1880 and died in London months later.

Eliot's first fiction, Scenes of Clerical Life, was followed by Adam Bede, The Mill on the Floss and Silas Marner. Middlemarch, published in 1871 and 1872, portrays the lives of a provincial town with extraordinary breadth and sympathy, following Dorothea Brooke, the doctor Lydgate and many others. Her final novel, Daniel Deronda, explores Jewish identity. Eliot combined realism with deep moral insight, and Middlemarch is often named among the greatest novels in English.

How it begins

In the days when the spinning-wheels hummed busily in the farmhouses—and even great ladies, clothed in silk and thread-lace, had their toy spinning-wheels of polished oak—there might be seen in districts far away among the lanes, or deep in the bosom of the hills, certain pallid undersized men, who, by the side of the brawny country-folk, looked like the remnants of a disinherited race. The shepherd’s dog barked fiercely when one of these alien-looking men appeared on the upland, dark against the early winter sunset; for what dog likes a figure bent under a heavy bag?—and these pale men rarely stirred abroad without that mysterious burden. The shepherd himself, though he had good reason to believe that the bag held nothing but flaxen thread, or else the long rolls of strong linen spun from that thread, was not quite sure that this trade of weaving, indispensable though it was, could be carried on entirely without the help of the Evil One. In that far-off time superstition clung easily round every person or thing that was at all unwonted, or even intermittent and occasional merely, like the visits of the pedlar or the knife-grinder. No one knew where wandering men had their homes or their origin; and how was a man to be explained unless you at least knew somebody who knew his father and mother?
