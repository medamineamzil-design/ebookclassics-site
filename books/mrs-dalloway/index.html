About the book

The novel follows a single day in June 1923 in London. Clarissa Dalloway, the wife of a Conservative member of Parliament, walks out to buy flowers for the party she is giving that evening. As she moves through the city, her thoughts drift back to her youth at Bourton, to her old suitor Peter Walsh, who unexpectedly reappears that morning, and to her friend Sally Seton. Running alongside her story is that of Septimus Warren Smith, a young veteran of the First World War suffering from shell shock, and his Italian wife Lucrezia, who are seeking help from doctors who barely understand him.

Woolf moves fluidly in and out of the minds of many characters, linking them through shared moments such as the striking of Big Ben. The book explores time and memory, the aftermath of war, mental illness, social class and the hidden depths behind ordinary lives. Its lyrical, flowing style rewards slow reading and shows how much can happen within an apparently uneventful day.

Mrs Dalloway was published in 1925 by the Hogarth Press, which Woolf ran with her husband Leonard. It is regarded as one of the central works of literary modernism. Woolf's working title was The Hours, a phrase Michael Cunningham later used for his own novel inspired by the book. It has also been adapted for film and the stage.

About Virginia Woolf

Adeline Virginia Stephen was born in London in 1882, the daughter of the critic Sir Leslie Stephen. Denied the university education given to her brothers, she educated herself in her father's library. The deaths of her mother, half-sister, father and brother brought grief and breakdowns. Her Bloomsbury home with her siblings became a centre of the Bloomsbury Group. In 1912 she married Leonard Woolf, and together they founded the Hogarth Press. Suffering another severe depression, she drowned herself in the River Ouse in Sussex in 1941.

Mrs Dalloway, following a London hostess through a single June day, and To the Lighthouse use stream of consciousness to capture the flow of thought. Orlando follows a hero who lives for centuries and changes sex. In the essay A Room of One's Own she argued that a woman needs money and a room of her own to write. Her innovations and feminist thought make her one of the central modernist writers.

How it begins

What a lark! What a plunge! For so it had always seemed to her, when, with a little squeak of the hinges, which she could hear now, she had burst open the French windows and plunged at Bourton into the open air. How fresh, how calm, stiller than this of course, the air was in the early morning; like the flap of a wave; the kiss of a wave; chill and sharp and yet (for a girl of eighteen as she then was) solemn, feeling as she did, standing there at the open window, that something awful was about to happen; looking at the flowers, at the trees with the smoke winding off them and the rooks rising, falling; standing and looking until Peter Walsh said, “Musing among the vegetables?”--was that it?--“I prefer men to cauliflowers”--was that it? He must have said it at breakfast one morning when she had gone out on to the terrace--Peter Walsh. He would be back from India one of these days, June or July, she forgot which, for his letters were awfully dull; it was his sayings one remembered; his eyes, his pocket-knife, his smile, his grumpiness and, when millions of things had utterly vanished--how strange it was!--a few sayings like this about cabbages.

She stiffened a little on the kerb, waiting for Durtnall’s van to pass. A charming woman, Scrope Purvis thought her (knowing her as one does know people who live next door to one in Westminster); a touch of the bird about her, of the jay, blue-green, light, vivacious, though she was over fifty, and grown very white since her illness. There she perched, never seeing him, waiting to cross, very upright.

For having lived in Westminster--how many years now? over twenty,--one feels even in the midst of the traffic, or waking at night, Clarissa was positive, a particular hush, or solemnity; an indescribable pause; a suspense (but that might be her heart, affected, they said, by influenza) before Big Ben strikes. There! Out it boomed. First a warning, musical; then the hour, irrevocable. The leaden circles dissolved in the air. Such fools we are, she thought, crossing Victoria Street. For Heaven only knows why one loves it so, how one sees it so, making it up, building it round one, tumbling it, creating it every moment afresh; but the veriest frumps, the most dejected of miseries sitting on doorsteps (drink their downfall) do the same; can’t be dealt with, she felt positive, by Acts of Parliament for that very reason: they love life. In people’s eyes, in the swing, tramp, and trudge; in the bellow and the uproar; the carriages, motor cars, omnibuses, vans, sandwich men shuffling and swinging; brass bands; barrel organs; in the triumph and the jingle and the strange high singing of some aeroplane overhead was what she loved; life; London; this moment of June.
