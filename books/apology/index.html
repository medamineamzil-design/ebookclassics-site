About the book

In 399 BC the philosopher Socrates stands before a jury of Athenian citizens, accused by Meletus, Anytus and Lycon of failing to honour the city's gods, introducing new divinities and corrupting the young. In his defence he explains how the oracle at Delphi declared that no one was wiser than he, and how his attempts to understand this led him to question politicians, poets and craftsmen, revealing that they knew less than they believed.

Plato's account of the trial is both a moving portrait of his teacher and a powerful statement of the philosophical life: the duty to seek truth, to examine oneself and to act justly whatever the consequences. Socrates compares himself to a gadfly sent to keep the city awake.

The Apology is Plato's version of the speech Socrates gave at his trial in 399 BC, after which he was condemned to death. This edition offers the classic English translation by Benjamin Jowett, with his introduction.

About Plato

Plato was born in Athens around 428 BC into an aristocratic family. As a young man he followed the philosopher Socrates, whose execution in 399 BC turned him away from politics. He visited Sicily, where he tried without success to influence the ruler of Syracuse. Around 387 BC he founded the Academy in Athens, where Aristotle later studied. He taught and wrote there until his death around 348 BC.

Plato wrote dialogues, most featuring Socrates questioning others about justice, virtue, love, knowledge and the soul. The Apology, Crito and Phaedo portray Socrates' trial and death; the Symposium explores love; and the Republic describes an ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings and includes the famous allegory of the cave. Central to his thought is the theory of Forms, the idea that perfect, unchanging realities lie behind the world we perceive. His works shaped philosophy, theology and politics, making him one of the most influential thinkers in history.

How it begins

In what relation the “Apology” of Plato stands to the real defence of Socrates, there are no means of determining. It certainly agrees in tone and character with the description of Xenophon, who says in the “Memorabilia” that Socrates might have been acquitted “if in any moderate degree he would have conciliated the favour of the dicasts;” and who informs us in another passage, on the testimony of Hermogenes, the friend of Socrates, that he had no wish to live; and that the divine sign refused to allow him to prepare a defence, and also that Socrates himself declared this to be unnecessary, on the ground that all his life long he had been preparing against that hour. For the speech breathes throughout a spirit of defiance, “ut non supplex aut reus sed magister aut dominus videretur esse judicum” (Cic. “de Orat.” i. 54); and the loose and desultory style is an imitation of the “accustomed manner” in which Socrates spoke in “the agora and among the tables of the money-changers.” The allusion in the “Crito” (45 B) may, perhaps, be adduced as a further evidence of the literal accuracy of some parts (37 C, D). But in the main it must be regarded as the ideal of Socrates, according to Plato’s conception of him, appearing in the greatest and most public scene of his life, and in the height of his triumph, when he is weakest, and yet his mastery over mankind is greatest, and his habitual irony acquires a new meaning and a sort of tragic pathos in the face of death. Yet some of the topics may have been actually used by Socrates; and the recollection of his very words may have rung in the ears of his disciple. The “Apology” of Plato may be compared generally with those speeches of Thucydides in which he has embodied his conception of the lofty character and policy of the great Pericles, and which at the same time furnish a commentary on the situation of affairs from the point of view of the historian. So in the “Apology” there is an ideal rather than a literal truth; much is said which was not said, and is only Plato’s view of the situation. Plato was not, like Xenophon, a chronicler of facts; he does not appear in any of his writings to have aimed at literal accuracy. He is not therefore to be supplemented from the Memorabilia and Symposium of Xenophon, who belongs to an entirely different class of writers. The Apology of Plato is not the report of what Socrates said, but an elaborate composition, quite as much so in fact as one of the Dialogues. And we may perhaps even indulge in the fancy that the actual defence of Socrates was as much greater than the Platonic defence as the master was greater than the disciple. But in any case, some of the words used by him must have been remembered, and some of the facts recorded must have actually occurred.
