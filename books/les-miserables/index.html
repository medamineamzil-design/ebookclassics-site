About the book

Les Misérables follows Jean Valjean, a man released after nineteen years in prison for stealing bread and later trying to escape. Hardened and shunned, he is transformed by an unexpected act of kindness from the Bishop of Digne and resolves to live an honest life. Yet the police inspector Javert, who believes no criminal can change, never stops hunting him. Ranging from 1815 to the Paris uprising of June 1832, the novel weaves Valjean's fate together with those of the struggling factory worker Fantine, her daughter Cosette, the scheming Thénardier family and the young idealist Marius.

Hugo's great subjects are justice and mercy, poverty and the law, and the possibility of moral rebirth. He writes on an enormous scale, pausing the story for long reflections on Waterloo, the Paris sewers, convents and the street children of the city. These digressions give the book its sense of an entire society in motion. Its compassion for the poor and its question of whether people can truly change remain as urgent as ever.

Hugo wrote much of the novel while living in political exile on Guernsey, and it appeared in 1862 in several volumes, published simultaneously in a number of countries. It was an immediate popular event. It has since been translated into many languages and adapted countless times for film, television, radio and the stage, most famously as a long-running musical.

First published in French in 1862, in five volumes.

Les Misérables follows Jean Valjean, a former convict imprisoned nineteen years for stealing bread, across several decades of nineteenth-century France as he struggles to escape both poverty and the relentless pursuit of the law. Transformed by the unexpected mercy of a bishop who gives him stolen silver rather than turning him in, Valjean breaks his parole, assumes a new identity, and eventually becomes a wealthy factory owner and respected mayor under the name Madeleine, using his fortune for charity. He is dogged throughout by Inspector Javert, a police officer whose rigid, unbending belief in law and order makes him incapable of recognizing redemption or circumstance, only guilt and its punishment. When a innocent man is mistakenly arrested as the real Valjean, the mayor confesses his true identity rather than let another suffer in his place, sacrificing his security. He then dedicates himself to caring for Fantine, a dying factory worker who was dismissed and driven to prostitution after concealing an illegitimate daughter, Cosette, who is being cruelly exploited by the corrupt innkeepers the Thénardiers. Valjean rescues Cosette, raises her as his own, and the two live in hiding in Paris and a convent to escape Javert's pursuit. Years later, as a young woman, Cosette falls in love with Marius, an idealistic student drawn into the republican uprising of the 1832 Paris rebellion, staged around a doomed barricade. Valjean, initially wary of losing Cosette, ultimately risks his life during the uprising, saving the wounded Marius by carrying him through the Paris sewers. Javert, captured by the rebels and spared execution by Valjean himself, is so shattered by this act of mercy from a man he has hunted for decades, mercy that contradicts his entire worldview, that he takes his own life. Valjean, having secured Cosette and Marius's marriage and happiness, withdraws from their lives out of guilt over his past, growing weaker until the couple discovers the truth and rushes to his side as he dies, at peace, surrounded by love he long believed himself unworthy of.

About Victor Hugo

Victor Hugo was born in Besançon in 1802, the son of an officer in Napoleon's army. He won early fame as a poet, and his play Hernani in 1830 marked the triumph of Romanticism in French theatre. The drowning of his daughter Léopoldine in 1843 devastated him. He opposed Napoleon III's seizure of power in 1851 and spent nearly twenty years in exile, mostly on Guernsey. He returned in 1870 and, when he died in Paris in 1885, was buried in the Panthéon.

Hugo's novel The Hunchback of Notre-Dame, published in 1831, tells of Quasimodo and Esmeralda in medieval Paris and helped inspire the restoration of the cathedral. Les Misérables, completed in exile and published in 1862, follows the former convict Jean Valjean through decades of French history, pleading for justice, mercy and the poor. He also wrote poetry, plays and further novels, and campaigned against the death penalty. He is regarded as one of France's greatest writers.

Although this detail has no connection whatever with the real substance of what we are about to relate, it will not be superfluous, if merely for the sake of exactness in all points, to mention here the various rumors and remarks which had been in circulation about him from the very moment when he arrived in the diocese. True or false, that which is said of men often occupies as important a place in their lives, and above all in their destinies, as that which they do. M. Myriel was the son of a councillor of the Parliament of Aix; hence he belonged to the nobility of the bar. It was said that his father, destining him to be the heir of his own post, had married him at a very early age, eighteen or twenty, in accordance with a custom which is rather widely prevalent in parliamentary families. In spite of this marriage, however, it was said that Charles Myriel created a great deal of talk. He was well formed, though rather short in stature, elegant, graceful, intelligent; the whole of the first portion of his life had been devoted to the world and to gallantry.

The Revolution came; events succeeded each other with precipitation; the parliamentary families, decimated, pursued, hunted down, were dispersed. M. Charles Myriel emigrated to Italy at the very beginning of the Revolution. There his wife died of a malady of the chest, from which she had long suffered. He had no children. What took place next in the fate of M. Myriel? The ruin of the French society of the olden days, the fall of his own family, the tragic spectacles of ’93, which were, perhaps, even more alarming to the emigrants who viewed them from a distance, with the magnifying powers of terror,—did these cause the ideas of renunciation and solitude to germinate in him? Was he, in the midst of these distractions, these affections which absorbed his life, suddenly smitten with one of those mysterious and terrible blows which sometimes overwhelm, by striking to his heart, a man whom public catastrophes would not shake, by striking at his existence and his fortune? No one could have told: all that was known was, that when he returned from Italy he was a priest.

In 1804, M. Myriel was the Curé of B—— [Brignolles]. He was already advanced in years, and lived in a very retired manner.
