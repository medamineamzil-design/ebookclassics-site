About the book

The Republic is a dialogue in which Socrates, visiting the port of Piraeus near Athens, is drawn into a conversation about the meaning of justice. After he challenges the claim of the sophist Thrasymachus that justice is simply the advantage of the stronger, Plato's brothers Glaucon and Adeimantus press him to prove that a just life is better than an unjust one, even if no one rewards it. To answer, Socrates imagines building an ideal city, reasoning that justice may be easier to see on a larger scale.

Across ten books the dialogue covers an extraordinary range of ideas: a city divided into rulers, guardians and producers, mirrored by a soul divided into reason, spirit and appetite; the rule of philosopher-kings; the education of the guardians; the censorship of poetry; the theory of Forms; and the famous allegory of the cave, which pictures human beings mistaking shadows for reality. Some proposals, such as communal living for the guardians, are startling or troubling, and part of the value of reading the work is arguing with it. It remains a lively introduction to questions of ethics, politics, knowledge and education.

Written in the fourth century BC, the Republic has been studied continuously since antiquity. It has shaped Western philosophy, political theory and utopian writing more deeply than almost any other text, and it remains central to university courses around the world.

Written in dialogue form, likely composed over several years around 375 BC.

About Plato

Plato was born in Athens around 428 BC into an aristocratic family. As a young man he followed the philosopher Socrates, whose execution in 399 BC turned him away from politics. He visited Sicily, where he tried without success to influence the ruler of Syracuse. Around 387 BC he founded the Academy in Athens, where Aristotle later studied. He taught and wrote there until his death around 348 BC.

Plato wrote dialogues, most featuring Socrates questioning others about justice, virtue, love, knowledge and the soul. The Apology, Crito and Phaedo portray Socrates' trial and death; the Symposium explores love; and the Republic describes an ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings and includes the famous allegory of the cave. Central to his thought is the theory of Forms, the idea that perfect, unchanging realities lie behind the world we perceive. His works shaped philosophy, theology and politics, making him one of the most influential thinkers in history.
