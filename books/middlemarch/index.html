About the book

Middlemarch, subtitled A Study of Provincial Life, is set in a fictional Midlands town in the years around 1830, a time of political reform, new railways and changing medicine. At its heart is Dorothea Brooke, an idealistic young woman who longs to do something meaningful with her life and chooses to marry the much older scholar Edward Casaubon. Alongside her story runs that of Tertius Lydgate, an ambitious young doctor eager to modernise medicine, who is drawn to the charming Rosamond Vincy. Around them a whole community comes to life, including Rosamond's easygoing brother Fred, the sensible Mary Garth, the young artist Will Ladislaw and the pious banker Bulstrode.

Eliot follows how hopes, money, gossip and marriage shape these lives, showing how small choices can have large consequences. Her narrator is wise, ironic and deeply compassionate, inviting readers to understand rather than judge. The novel's insight into ambition, compromise and the limits placed on women still feels remarkably fresh.

George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans. Middlemarch was first issued in eight parts in 1871 and 1872 and was well received. Its reputation has grown steadily, and many writers and critics regard it as one of the finest novels in English. It has been adapted for television and radio and is widely studied.

About George Eliot

Mary Ann Evans was born in 1819 on an estate in Warwickshire, where her father worked as land agent. A brilliant student, she lost her evangelical faith as a young woman, causing a painful rift with her father. She translated German theological works and moved to London, where she became assistant editor of the Westminster Review. She lived openly for over twenty years with the married writer George Henry Lewes, which scandalised society. She married John Cross in 1880 and died in London months later.

Eliot's first fiction, Scenes of Clerical Life, was followed by Adam Bede, The Mill on the Floss and Silas Marner. Middlemarch, published in 1871 and 1872, portrays the lives of a provincial town with extraordinary breadth and sympathy, following Dorothea Brooke, the doctor Lydgate and many others. Her final novel, Daniel Deronda, explores Jewish identity. Eliot combined realism with deep moral insight, and Middlemarch is often named among the greatest novels in English.

How it begins

Miss Brooke had that kind of beauty which seems to be thrown into relief by poor dress. Her hand and wrist were so finely formed that she could wear sleeves not less bare of style than those in which the Blessed Virgin appeared to Italian painters; and her profile as well as her stature and bearing seemed to gain the more dignity from her plain garments, which by the side of provincial fashion gave her the impressiveness of a fine quotation from the Bible,—or from one of our elder poets,—in a paragraph of to-day’s newspaper. She was usually spoken of as being remarkably clever, but with the addition that her sister Celia had more common-sense. Nevertheless, Celia wore scarcely more trimmings; and it was only to close observers that her dress differed from her sister’s, and had a shade of coquetry in its arrangements; for Miss Brooke’s plain dressing was due to mixed conditions, in most of which her sister shared. The pride of being ladies had something to do with it: the Brooke connections, though not exactly aristocratic, were unquestionably “good:” if you inquired backward for a generation or two, you would not find any yard-measuring or parcel-tying forefathers—anything lower than an admiral or a clergyman; and there was even an ancestor discernible as a Puritan gentleman who served under Cromwell, but afterwards conformed, and managed to come out of all political troubles as the proprietor of a respectable family estate.
