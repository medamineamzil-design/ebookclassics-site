About the book

The Prince is a short treatise on how a ruler can win power and keep it. Machiavelli, a former official of the Florentine republic who lost his post when the Medici family returned to power in 1512, wrote it in 1513 and dedicated it to Lorenzo de' Medici. He begins by sorting principalities into types, hereditary and new, won by one's own arms or by those of others, and then turns to armies, the conduct of princes, and the condition of Italy in his own time.

What makes the book famous is its frankness. Machiavelli sets aside the traditional advice that rulers should simply be virtuous and asks instead what actually works, drawing on examples from ancient Rome and from recent figures such as Cesare Borgia. He discusses whether it is better to be loved or feared, how far a ruler should keep promises, and the struggle between a leader's skill, which he calls virtù, and the unpredictable force of fortune. Its clear, compact arguments still provoke debate about the relationship between ethics and politics.

The book circulated in manuscript during Machiavelli's lifetime and was printed only in 1532, five years after his death. It soon became notorious, and it was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of prohibited books in 1559. The word "Machiavellian" entered many languages as a byword for cunning, yet scholars have long read the book as a founding work of modern political science.

Written around 1513; published posthumously in 1532.

About Niccolò Machiavelli

Niccolò Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469, the son of a lawyer, and received a humanist education. In 1498 he became secretary of the Florentine republic's second chancery and was sent on missions to the king of France, Cesare Borgia and the papal court. When the Medici returned in 1512 he lost his post, and the following year he was imprisoned and tortured on suspicion of conspiracy. Released, he retired to his farm outside Florence. He died in 1527.

In retirement Machiavelli wrote The Prince, a short guide for a new ruler, which circulated in manuscript and was published in 1532, after his death. It argues that a ruler must sometimes act ruthlessly, judging actions by their results rather than conventional morality. His Discourses on Livy praise republican government, and he also wrote the comedy The Mandrake. The word Machiavellian came to mean cunning, but his realistic analysis of politics made him a founder of modern political science.

How it begins

I will leave out all discussion on republics, inasmuch as in another place I have written of them at length, and will address myself only to principalities. In doing so I will keep to the order indicated above, and discuss how such principalities are to be ruled and preserved.

I say at once there are fewer difficulties in holding hereditary states, and those long accustomed to the family of their prince, than new ones; for it is sufficient only not to transgress the customs of his ancestors, and to deal prudently with circumstances as they arise, for a prince of average powers to maintain himself in his state, unless he be deprived of it by some extraordinary and excessive force; and if he should be so deprived of it, whenever anything sinister happens to the usurper, he will regain it.

We have in Italy, for example, the Duke of Ferrara, who could not have withstood the attacks of the Venetians in ’84, nor those of Pope Julius in ’10, unless he had been long established in his dominions. For the hereditary prince has less cause and less necessity to offend; hence it happens that he will be more loved; and unless extraordinary vices cause him to be hated, it is reasonable to expect that his subjects will be naturally well disposed towards him; and in the antiquity and duration of his rule the memories and motives that make for change are lost, for one change always leaves the toothing for another.
