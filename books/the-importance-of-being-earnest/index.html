About the book

Jack Worthing is a respectable guardian in the country, but in London he calls himself Ernest and enjoys a freer life. His friend Algernon Moncrieff has his own escape, an invented invalid named Bunbury whose sudden illnesses excuse him from dull engagements. Jack wants to marry Algernon's cousin Gwendolen, who adores the name Ernest, but must first satisfy her formidable mother, Lady Bracknell. When Algernon visits Jack's country house pretending to be Ernest himself, and falls for Jack's young ward Cecily, the double lives begin to collide.

Subtitled A Trivial Comedy for Serious People, the play turns Victorian ideas of duty, marriage, class and respectability upside down. Wilde treats trivial matters with great seriousness and serious matters with airy indifference, and nearly every line is an epigram. Its absurd plot, involving cucumber sandwiches, a handbag and a lost identity, moves with clockwork precision. It remains one of the funniest plays in English and a model of comic dialogue.

The play opened at the St James's Theatre in London on 14 February 1895 to great success. Within weeks Wilde's legal troubles began, and after his arrest his name was removed from publicity and the run soon ended. It was published in 1899. Since then it has been revived constantly on stage and adapted for film, radio, television and opera.

About Oscar Wilde

Oscar Wilde was born in Dublin in 1854, the son of a celebrated surgeon and a nationalist poet. He studied classics at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford, becoming a voice of aestheticism, the belief in art for art's sake. He lectured across America in 1882 and married Constance Lloyd, with whom he had two sons. In 1895, after a failed libel case against the father of his lover Lord Alfred Douglas, he was sentenced to two years' hard labour for gross indecency. He died in poverty in Paris in 1900.

Wilde wrote fairy tales such as The Happy Prince. The Picture of Dorian Gray, his only novel, tells of a young man whose portrait ages while he stays beautiful. His comedies, including An Ideal Husband and The Importance of Being Earnest, sparkle with wit while mocking Victorian society. Prison produced the long letter De Profundis and The Ballad of Reading Gaol. His epigrams are still quoted everywhere.
