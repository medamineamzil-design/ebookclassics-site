About the book

The Iliad is set in the tenth year of the Greek siege of Troy, but it covers only a few weeks of the war. It opens with a bitter quarrel: Agamemnon, leader of the Greek army, takes from Achilles, the greatest of the Greek warriors, a captive woman awarded to him as a prize of honour. Furious and humiliated, Achilles withdraws from the fighting, and his mother, the sea goddess Thetis, persuades Zeus to let the Trojans gain the upper hand. As the battle turns, heroes on both sides, among them the Trojan prince Hector, fight, boast, grieve and die, while the gods of Olympus quarrel and intervene.

Although it is a poem of war, the Iliad is also deeply concerned with what war costs. Anger, pride, honour and grief drive its action, and it gives as much dignity to the Trojans as to the victors. Its portraits of friendship and loss remain moving and immediate.

Composed in Greek hexameter verse and traditionally attributed to Homer, the poem is usually dated to around the eighth century BC and was later divided into twenty-four books. It became a cornerstone of Greek education and has shaped Western literature ever since. It has been translated countless times, including a famous English verse version by Alexander Pope, and retold in novels, plays and films.

About Homer

Nothing about Homer's life can be known for certain. The ancient Greeks believed he was a blind poet from Ionia, on the coast of Asia Minor, and several cities, including Smyrna and Chios, claimed him as their own. Modern scholars debate whether a single poet composed both great epics or whether they grew out of a long tradition of oral storytelling. The poems probably took shape in the eighth or seventh century BC and were written down later.

The Iliad tells of the anger of Achilles during the final year of the Trojan War and its tragic consequences for Greeks and Trojans alike. The Odyssey follows the clever Odysseus on his long journey home to Ithaca and his wife Penelope after the war. Composed in hexameter verse, rich in vivid similes and repeated epithets, the poems were central to Greek education. They inspired Virgil's Aeneid, countless later poets and artists, and modern works such as James Joyce's Ulysses.
