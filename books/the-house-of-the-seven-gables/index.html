About the book

In the seventeenth century, Colonel Pyncheon seized the land of Matthew Maule, a poor man executed for witchcraft, and built a grand house with seven gables on it; Maule cursed him from the scaffold. Two centuries later, the house has decayed along with the family's fortunes. The proud, impoverished old spinster Hepzibah Pyncheon is forced to open a little cent-shop to support her brother Clifford, newly released after thirty years in prison, while their powerful cousin Judge Jaffrey Pyncheon watches them, and a young country cousin, Phoebe, and a mysterious lodger, Holgrave, bring new life to the house.

Hawthorne explores how the sins and greed of one generation weigh on the next, mixing Gothic mystery, gentle humour and moral reflection. The old house itself, with its shadows, portraits and secrets, becomes a central character.

The House of the Seven Gables was published in 1851 and is one of Hawthorne's most celebrated works. It is set in Salem, Massachusetts.

About Nathaniel Hawthorne

Nathaniel Hawthorne was born in 1804 in Salem, Massachusetts. One of his Puritan ancestors had been a judge at the Salem witch trials, a legacy that troubled him. His father, a sea captain, died when Nathaniel was a child. At Bowdoin College his classmates included the poet Longfellow and the future president Franklin Pierce. He spent years writing in seclusion, briefly joined the utopian community of Brook Farm, and married Sophia Peabody. He worked at the Salem Custom House and as US consul in Liverpool, and died in 1864.

His stories include Young Goodman Brown and Rappaccini's Daughter. The Scarlet Letter, published in 1850, tells of Hester Prynne, forced to wear a scarlet A for adultery in Puritan Boston, and of the hidden guilt of her lover, the minister Dimmesdale. It was followed by The House of the Seven Gables. Hawthorne explored sin, guilt and the weight of the past, and he influenced Herman Melville, who dedicated Moby-Dick to him.

How it begins

In September of the year during the February of which Hawthorne had completed “The Scarlet Letter,” he began “The House of the Seven Gables.” Meanwhile, he had removed from Salem to Lenox, in Berkshire County, Massachusetts, where he occupied with his family a small red wooden house, still standing at the date of this edition, near the Stockbridge Bowl.

“I sha’n’t have the new story ready by November,” he explained to his publisher, on the 1st of October, “for I am never good for anything in the literary way till after the first autumnal frost, which has somewhat such an effect on my imagination that it does on the foliage here about me—multiplying and brightening its hues.” But by vigorous application he was able to complete the new work about the middle of the January following.

Since research has disclosed the manner in which the romance is interwoven with incidents from the history of the Hawthorne family, “The House of the Seven Gables” has acquired an interest apart from that by which it first appealed to the public. John Hathorne (as the name was then spelled), the great-grandfather of Nathaniel Hawthorne, was a magistrate at Salem in the latter part of the seventeenth century, and officiated at the famous trials for witchcraft held there. It is of record that he used peculiar severity towards a certain woman who was among the accused; and the husband of this woman prophesied that God would take revenge upon his wife’s persecutors. This circumstance doubtless furnished a hint for that piece of tradition in the book which represents a Pyncheon of a former generation as having persecuted one Maule, who declared that God would give his enemy “blood to drink.” It became a conviction with the Hawthorne family that a curse had been pronounced upon its members, which continued in force in the time of the romancer; a conviction perhaps derived from the recorded prophecy of the injured woman’s husband, just mentioned; and, here again, we have a correspondence with Maule’s malediction in the story.
