About the book

Moby-Dick begins with a young man who asks to be called Ishmael heading to sea to escape his restlessness. In New Bedford he befriends Queequeg, a harpooner from the South Seas, and the two sign on to the Nantucket whaling ship Pequod. Only once the voyage is under way does the ship's captain, Ahab, appear on deck and reveal his true purpose: to hunt down the great white whale that took his leg on an earlier voyage. The crew, including the cautious first mate Starbuck, is drawn into his quest across the world's oceans.

Melville mixes adventure with an astonishing range of other forms: sermons, stage-like scenes, soliloquies, and detailed chapters on whales, whaling and the work of the ship. The book asks large questions about obsession, fate, the limits of knowledge and humanity's place in nature, while also offering humour, warmth and friendship across cultures. Readers who accept its digressions find one of the most ambitious and original novels ever written.

The novel appeared in 1851, first in London under the title The Whale and shortly afterwards in New York as Moby-Dick. It sold poorly and puzzled many reviewers, and Melville died in relative obscurity. In the early twentieth century critics rediscovered it, and it is now widely considered one of the great American novels. It has inspired films, stage works, artworks and countless references in popular culture.

About Herman Melville

Herman Melville was born in New York City in 1819. His father's business failed, and he died when Herman was twelve. After working as a clerk and teacher, Melville went to sea, eventually on a whaler in the Pacific. He deserted in the Marquesas Islands and returned home on a navy frigate. He married Elizabeth Shaw and befriended Nathaniel Hawthorne. Later he worked for nineteen years as a customs inspector in New York, where he died in 1891, largely forgotten.

Melville's first books, Typee and Omoo, drew on his South Sea adventures and were popular. Moby-Dick, published in 1851, tells of Captain Ahab's obsessive hunt for a white whale, mixing adventure, philosophy and whaling lore; it sold poorly. He went on to write stories such as Bartleby, the Scrivener, the novel The Confidence-Man, and Billy Budd, published only in 1924. A revival in the 1920s established Moby-Dick as one of the great American novels.

How it begins

Call me Ishmael. Some years ago—never mind how long precisely—having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world. It is a way I have of driving off the spleen and regulating the circulation. Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet; and especially whenever my hypos get such an upper hand of me, that it requires a strong moral principle to prevent me from deliberately stepping into the street, and methodically knocking people’s hats off—then, I account it high time to get to sea as soon as I can. This is my substitute for pistol and ball. With a philosophical flourish Cato throws himself upon his sword; I quietly take to the ship. There is nothing surprising in this. If they but knew it, almost all men in their degree, some time or other, cherish very nearly the same feelings towards the ocean with me.

There now is your insular city of the Manhattoes, belted round by wharves as Indian isles by coral reefs—commerce surrounds it with her surf. Right and left, the streets take you waterward. Its extreme downtown is the battery, where that noble mole is washed by waves, and cooled by breezes, which a few hours previous were out of sight of land. Look at the crowds of water-gazers there.

Circumambulate the city of a dreamy Sabbath afternoon. Go from Corlears Hook to Coenties Slip, and from thence, by Whitehall, northward. What do you see?—Posted like silent sentinels all around the town, stand thousands upon thousands of mortal men fixed in ocean reveries. Some leaning against the spiles; some seated upon the pier-heads; some looking over the bulwarks of ships from China; some high aloft in the rigging, as if striving to get a still better seaward peep. But these are all landsmen; of week days pent up in lath and plaster—tied to counters, nailed to benches, clinched to desks. How then is this? Are the green fields gone? What do they here?

But look! here come more crowds, pacing straight for the water, and seemingly bound for a dive. Strange! Nothing will content them but the extremest limit of the land; loitering under the shady lee of yonder warehouses will not suffice. No. They must get just as nigh the water as they possibly can without falling in. And there they stand—miles of them—leagues. Inlanders all, they come from lanes and alleys, streets and avenues—north, east, south, and west. Yet here they all unite. Tell me, does the magnetic virtue of the needles of the compasses of all those ships attract them thither?
