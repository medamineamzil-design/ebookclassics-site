About the book

Frankenstein opens with letters from Robert Walton, an Arctic explorer who takes aboard a half-frozen stranger named Victor Frankenstein. Victor tells his story: a gifted young man from Geneva, he becomes obsessed at university with discovering the secret of life, and succeeds in animating a creature built from dead matter. Horrified by what he has made, he flees from it. The creature, left alone in a hostile world, learns language and feeling by secretly observing a family, and eventually confronts its maker with demands of its own.

Mary Shelley's novel is far more than a horror story. It explores the dangers of unchecked scientific ambition, the duties a creator owes to what he creates, and the way rejection and loneliness can twist a sensitive being. Unusually, the creature is given a voice of its own, eloquent and moving, which complicates any simple idea of who the real monster is. Its urgent moral questions feel fresh in an age of genetic engineering and artificial intelligence.

The novel grew out of a ghost-story challenge among Mary Shelley, Percy Bysshe Shelley and Lord Byron near Lake Geneva in 1816. Subtitled The Modern Prometheus, it was published anonymously in London in 1818, and a revised edition followed in 1831. It is often called one of the first works of science fiction. Stage versions appeared within a few years, and countless adaptations have followed.

Frankenstein is framed as a series of letters from the Arctic explorer Robert Walton to his sister, recounting his rescue of a half-frozen, grief-stricken man, Victor Frankenstein, who then narrates his own history within Walton's account. Victor describes an idyllic childhood in Geneva, his fascination with outdated alchemical texts, and his departure for university at Ingolstadt, where modern chemistry supplants his old enthusiasms and he becomes obsessed with the secret of animating dead matter. Working in isolation, he assembles a creature from scavenged body parts and, in a moment of triumph immediately curdling into horror, brings it to life, only to flee in disgust at its appearance. The abandoned creature, nameless throughout the novel, wanders alone, learning language and human feeling by secretly observing a poor cottage family, the De Laceys, and reading discarded books, including Paradise Lost, through which he comes to see himself as a kind of fallen, unloved Adam. Rejected with violence whenever he approaches people, including the cottagers he had grown to love, the creature's initial gentleness hardens into vengeful rage. He confronts Victor, demanding that his creator build him a female companion so he need not be alone; Victor agrees, then destroys the half-finished female in a fit of moral horror, provoking the creature's furious vow: "I will be with you on your wedding night." The creature then murders Victor's young brother William, and William's nursemaid Justine is wrongfully executed for the crime, followed later by the murders of Victor's friend Clerval and, on Victor's wedding night exactly as promised, his bride Elizabeth. Consumed by guilt and hatred, Victor pursues the creature into the Arctic wastes, where Walton finds him. Victor dies aboard Walton's ship still vowing vengeance, and the creature appears one last time, mourning his creator and creation alike before vanishing into the ice to end his own life, his purpose for existing having been entirely bound up with the man who made and then abandoned him.

About Mary Shelley

Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin was born in London in 1797, the daughter of the writer Mary Wollstonecraft, who died days after her birth, and the philosopher William Godwin. At sixteen she eloped with the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley. In the summer of 1816, near Lord Byron by Lake Geneva, a ghost-story contest gave her the idea for her first novel, and the couple married that December. Several of her children died young; in 1822 Percy drowned off Italy. Back in England, she supported herself and her son by writing. She died in London in 1851.

Frankenstein was published anonymously in 1818, when Mary was twenty. It tells of Victor Frankenstein, a young scientist who creates a living being and then abandons it, with tragic results. Exploring ambition, responsibility and loneliness, it is often seen as one of the first science fiction novels. She also wrote The Last Man, about a plague that destroys humanity. Her creature remains one of the most enduring figures of modern myth.

How it begins

You will rejoice to hear that no disaster has accompanied the commencement of an enterprise which you have regarded with such evil forebodings. I arrived here yesterday, and my first task is to assure my dear sister of my welfare and increasing confidence in the success of my undertaking.

I am already far north of London, and as I walk in the streets of Petersburgh, I feel a cold northern breeze play upon my cheeks, which braces my nerves and fills me with delight. Do you understand this feeling? This breeze, which has travelled from the regions towards which I am advancing, gives me a foretaste of those icy climes. Inspirited by this wind of promise, my daydreams become more fervent and vivid. I try in vain to be persuaded that the pole is the seat of frost and desolation; it ever presents itself to my imagination as the region of beauty and delight. There, Margaret, the sun is for ever visible, its broad disk just skirting the horizon and diffusing a perpetual splendour. There—for with your leave, my sister, I will put some trust in preceding navigators—there snow and frost are banished; and, sailing over a calm sea, we may be wafted to a land surpassing in wonders and in beauty every region hitherto discovered on the habitable globe. Its productions and features may be without example, as the phenomena of the heavenly bodies undoubtedly are in those undiscovered solitudes. What may not be expected in a country of eternal light? I may there discover the wondrous power which attracts the needle and may regulate a thousand celestial observations that require only this voyage to render their seeming eccentricities consistent for ever. I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never befor…

These reflections have dispelled the agitation with which I began my letter, and I feel my heart glow with an enthusiasm which elevates me to heaven, for nothing contributes so much to tranquillise the mind as a steady purpose—a point on which the soul may fix its intellectual eye. This expedition has been the favourite dream of my early years. I have read with ardour the accounts of the various voyages which have been made in the prospect of arriving at the North Pacific Ocean through the seas which surround the pole. You may remember that a history of all the voyages made for purposes of discovery composed the whole of our good Uncle Thomas’ library. My education was neglected, yet I was passionately fond of reading. These volumes were my study day and night, and my familiarity with them increased that regret which I had felt, as a child, on learning that my father’s dying injunction had forbidden my uncle to allow me to embark in a seafaring life.
