About the book

Adam Smith's first book asks how human beings come to make moral judgements. His answer begins with sympathy, which he describes as our capacity to imagine ourselves in another person's situation and share, to some degree, what they feel. We approve of others' feelings and actions when we can enter into them, and disapprove when we cannot. To judge our own conduct, Smith argues, we learn to view ourselves through the eyes of an imagined impartial spectator, a figure who gradually becomes the voice of conscience. From this foundation he discusses virtue, justice, beneficence, duty and the role of rules in moral life.

The book is notable for grounding ethics in social experience and emotion rather than pure reason or religious command. Smith writes with clarity and a keen eye for everyday behaviour, examining ambition, vanity, admiration for the rich and the human desire to be worthy of praise. It corrects the image of Smith as a champion of mere self-interest.

Smith published the work in 1759 while professor of moral philosophy at the University of Glasgow, and it made his reputation across Europe. It shows the influence of his friend David Hume. He revised it repeatedly, with a substantially expanded sixth edition appearing in 1790, the year of his death. Scholars continue to study how it connects with The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776.

About Adam Smith

Adam Smith was born in 1723 in Kirkcaldy, a small port town in Fife, Scotland. He studied at the University of Glasgow and at Balliol College, Oxford. After giving public lectures in Edinburgh, he became professor of logic and later of moral philosophy at Glasgow. In the 1760s he toured France as tutor to the young Duke of Buccleuch, meeting leading French thinkers. He spent his last years as a commissioner of customs in Edinburgh, where he died in 1790.

Smith published The Theory of Moral Sentiments in 1759, exploring how sympathy and the judgment of an imagined impartial spectator shape our moral lives. An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations followed in 1776, analysing the division of labour, markets, free trade and the proper role of government. Read together, his two great books reveal a thinker as concerned with ethics as with wealth, which is why economists, philosophers and historians still return to him.
