About the book

The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes collects twelve short stories that Arthur Conan Doyle published in The Strand Magazine between 1891 and 1892, all narrated by Holmes's friend and flatmate, Dr. John Watson, fresh off his own marriage and newly returned to visiting Baker Street. The volume opens with "A Scandal in Bohemia," in which the King of Bohemia hires Holmes to recover a compromising photograph from Irene Adler, a former opera singer whose wit ultimately outmaneuvers the detective himself, earning her the singular honor of being remembered as "the woman." "The Red-Headed League" follows a bewildered pawnbroker lured into a pointless clerical job by a society that turns out to be a cover for a bank-vault tunneling scheme, while "A Case of Identity" traces the disappearance of a bride from her own wedding breakfast to a domestic fraud closer to home than she suspects. Later stories send Holmes chasing a stolen blue carbuncle hidden inside a Christmas goose, protecting a young woman at a country house threatened by a venomous swamp adder disguised as a bell-rope, and unraveling a curse that has picked off the members of a family one by one, each preceded by an envelope of five dried orange pips. "The Man with the Twisted Lip" exposes a respectable-seeming beggar's double life, "The Adventure of the Engineer's Thumb" involves a counterfeiting operation and a hydraulic press turned murder weapon, and "The Adventure of the Noble Bachelor" and "The Beryl Coronet" both hinge on family secrets that Holmes must extract as delicately as the crime itself. The collection closes with "The Adventure of the Copper Beeches," in which a governess's oddly specific new job — including a request that she cut her hair and wear a particular blue dress — conceals a father imprisoning his own daughter. Across all twelve, the pattern repeats with satisfying regularity: a client arrives at 221B Baker Street with a problem that appears to defy explanation, and Holmes, working from physical evidence and a chain of deductions that Watson dutifully records but rarely anticipates, produces the solution before Scotland Yard has even settled on a plausible theory.

About Arthur Conan Doyle

British writer and physician (1859–1930), creator of the detective Sherlock Holmes, one of the most influential figures in crime fiction.

How it begins

To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman. I have seldom heard him mention her under any other name. In his eyes she eclipses and predominates the whole of her sex. It was not that he felt any emotion akin to love for Irene Adler. All emotions, and that one particularly, were abhorrent to his cold, precise but admirably balanced mind. He was, I take it, the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen, but as a lover he would have placed himself in a false position. He never spoke of the softer passions, save with a gibe and a sneer. They were admirable things for the observer—excellent for drawing the veil from men’s motives and actions. But for the trained reasoner to admit such intrusions into his own delicate and finely adjusted temperament was to introduce a distracting factor which might throw a doubt upon all his mental results. Grit in a sensitive instrument, or a crack in one of his own high-power lenses, would not be more disturbing than a strong emotion in a nature such as his.
