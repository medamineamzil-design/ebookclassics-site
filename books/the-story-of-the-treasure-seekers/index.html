About the book

After their mother's death and the failure of their father's business, the six Bastable children, Dora, Oswald, Dicky, Alice, Noel and Horace Octavius, known as H. O., live in a modest house on the Lewisham Road in London. Determined to restore the family fortunes, they try one plan after another: digging for treasure, becoming detectives, writing poetry for the newspapers, selling sherry and looking for a princess to rescue, often with the help, or hindrance, of Albert-next-door.

The story is told by one of the children, who claims not to reveal which one, but whose identity soon becomes obvious. Nesbit's humour and her realistic, unsentimental portrait of children at play give the book its lively voice.

The Bastable stories first appeared between 1894 and 1899 in several periodicals, among them The Pall Mall Magazine and the Windsor Magazine, and were collected in book form in 1899. Their adventures continue in The Wouldbegoods (1901).

About E. Nesbit

Edith Nesbit was born in 1858 in Kennington, London. Her father, an agricultural chemist, died when she was three. In 1880 she married Hubert Bland, and the couple were among the founders of the socialist Fabian Society. Their household was unconventional and often short of money, and Nesbit wrote poems, stories and journalism to support the family. Success came in her forties with books for children, published under the name E. Nesbit. She died in Kent in 1924.

The Story of the Treasure Seekers appeared in 1899, followed by Five Children and It, The Phoenix and the Carpet, The Railway Children and The Enchanted Castle. Nesbit wrote about lively, quarrelsome, believable children and placed magic in the middle of ordinary family life, often with comic results. Later fantasy writers, including C. S. Lewis, acknowledged her influence, and The Railway Children in particular has remained a much-loved classic on page and screen.

How it begins

There are some things I must tell before I begin to tell about the treasure-seeking, because I have read books myself, and I know how beastly it is when a story begins, “‘Alas!” said Hildegarde with a deep sigh, “we must look our last on this ancestral home”’--and then some one else says something--and you don’t know for pages and pages where the home is, or who Hildegarde is, or anything about it. Our ancestral home is in the Lewisham Road. It is semi-detached and has a garden, not a large one. We are the Bastables. There are six of us besides Father. Our Mother is dead, and if you think we don’t care because I don’t tell you much about her you only show that you do not understand people at all. Dora is the eldest. Then Oswald--and then Dicky. Oswald won the Latin prize at his preparatory school--and Dicky is good at sums. Alice and Noel are twins: they are ten, and Horace Octavius is my youngest brother. It is one of us that tells this story--but I shall not tell you which: only at the very end perhaps I will. While the story is going on you may be trying to guess, only I bet you don’t. It was Oswald who first thought of looking for treasure. Oswald often thinks of very interesting things. And directly he thought of it he did not keep it to himself, as some boys would have done, but he told the others, and said--

‘I’ll tell you what, we must go and seek for treasure: it is always what you do to restore the fallen fortunes of your House.’

Dora said it was all very well. She often says that. She was trying to mend a large hole in one of Noel’s stockings. He tore it on a nail when we were playing shipwrecked mariners on top of the chicken-house the day H. O. fell off and cut his chin: he has the scar still. Dora is the only one of us who ever tries to mend anything. Alice tries to make things sometimes. Once she knitted a red scarf for Noel because his chest is delicate, but it was much wider at one end than the other, and he wouldn’t wear it. So we used it as a pennon, and it did very well, because most of our things are black or grey since Mother died; and scarlet was a nice change. Father does not like you to ask for new things. That was one way we had of knowing that the fortunes of the ancient House of Bastable were really fallen. Another way was that there was no more pocket-money--except a penny now and then to the little ones, and people did not come to dinner any more, like they used to, with pretty dresses, driving up in cabs--and the carpets got holes in them--and when the legs came off things they were not sent to be mended, and we gave up having the gardener except for the front garden, and not that very often. And the silver in the big oak plate-chest that is lined with green baize all went away to the shop to have the dents and scratches taken out of it, and it never came back.
