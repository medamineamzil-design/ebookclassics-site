About the book

In Aci Trezza, a fishing village on the coast of Sicily near Catania, the Toscano family, nicknamed the Malavoglia, live in the house by the medlar tree. The household is led by the old patriarch Padron 'Ntoni, who speaks in proverbs and believes in hard work and family unity. Hoping to improve their fortunes, the family buys a cargo of lupins on credit and sends it out on their boat, the Provvidenza. When the venture goes badly wrong, they are left with a debt, and must struggle to keep their home and honour as the modern world presses in.

Verga tells the story almost without a narrator's voice, letting the gossip, sayings and viewpoints of the villagers carry the narrative. This choral, impersonal style was a bold innovation and is central to the movement known as verismo. The book is a moving portrait of poverty, dignity, change and the pull between tradition and the wish to escape, and its humane attention to ordinary people still feels fresh.

I Malavoglia was published in Milan in 1881 as the first part of a planned cycle about society's defeated, of which Verga completed only one further novel. It was poorly received at first but is now regarded as a masterpiece of Italian literature. It has been translated under this English title and loosely inspired Luchino Visconti's film La terra trema (1948).

First published in Italian as I Malavoglia in 1881.

About Giovanni Verga

Giovanni Verga was born in 1840 in Catania, Sicily, into a landowning family. He abandoned law studies to write, moving to Florence in 1865 and later to Milan. His early novels were sentimental, romantic stories, such as Story of a Blackcap. In later life he returned to Catania, where he lived quietly. He was made a senator in 1920 and died in his home town in 1922.

In the 1870s Verga turned to a new style, inspired partly by French naturalism, that became known as verismo. His stories in Life in the Fields, including Cavalleria rusticana, which Mascagni turned into a famous opera, and the novels I Malavoglia and Mastro-don Gesualdo portray Sicilian fishermen, peasants and landowners struggling against poverty and fate. Verga tried to let his characters speak for themselves, reducing the narrator's presence and using the rhythms of their speech. His impersonal technique and compassion for the defeated made him a key figure in modern Italian fiction.
