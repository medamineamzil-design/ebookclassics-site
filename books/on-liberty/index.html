About the book

On Liberty is a philosophical essay about the proper limits of power that society can rightfully exercise over the individual. Mill argues that the only good reason for interfering with someone's freedom, whether by law or by social pressure, is to prevent harm to others. A person's own good, physical or moral, is not a sufficient reason. From this starting point he defends freedom of thought and discussion, the freedom to live according to one's own tastes and plans, and the freedom to associate with others.

Mill was especially concerned that in democratic societies the danger came not only from governments but from the tyranny of the majority, the quiet pressure of custom and opinion that discourages people from thinking or living differently. He insists that even false opinions deserve to be heard, because truth is strengthened by being challenged, and that individuality and experimentation are essential to human progress. His prose is clear and closely reasoned, and his arguments remain central to modern debates about free speech, censorship and personal choice.

The essay was published in 1859, shortly after the death of Mill's wife, Harriet Taylor Mill, whom he credited as a close collaborator and to whose memory he dedicated it. It became one of the founding texts of liberal political thought and is still widely studied in philosophy, politics and law.

About John Stuart Mill

John Stuart Mill was born in London in 1806, the son of the philosopher James Mill, who gave him an intensive education beginning with Greek at three. At twenty he suffered a mental crisis and found relief in poetry, especially Wordsworth's. His long friendship with Harriet Taylor, whom he married in 1851, shaped his thinking. As a Member of Parliament he proposed giving women the vote. He died in Avignon, France, in 1873.

Mill's A System of Logic and Principles of Political Economy were major textbooks of their time. On Liberty, published in 1859, argues that society may limit individual freedom only to prevent harm to others and defends free thought and discussion. Utilitarianism refines Bentham's idea that actions should promote happiness, distinguishing higher from lower pleasures. The Subjection of Women makes a powerful case for equality between the sexes, and his Autobiography describes his unusual upbringing. His clear arguments for liberty and equality remain central to modern political debate.

On Liberty — Chapter I.

Introductory.

The subject of this Essay is not the so-called Liberty of the Will, so unfortunately opposed to the misnamed doctrine of Philosophical Necessity; but Civil, or Social Liberty: the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual. A question seldom stated, and hardly ever discussed, in general terms, but which profoundly influences the practical controversies of the age by its latent presence, and is likely soon to make itself recognised as the vital question of the future. It is so far from being new, that in a certain sense, it has divided mankind, almost from the remotest ages; but in the stage of progress into which the more civilised portions of the species have now entered, it presents itself under new conditions, and requires a different and more fundamental treatment.

The struggle between Liberty and Authority is the most conspicuous feature in the portions of history with which we are earliest familiar, particularly in that of Greece, Rome, and England. But in old times this contest was between subjects, or some classes of subjects, and the government. By liberty, was meant protection against the tyranny of the political rulers. The rulers were conceived (except in some of the popular governments of Greece) as in a necessarily antagonistic position to the people whom they ruled. They consisted of a governing One, or a governing tribe or caste, who derived their authority from inheritance or conquest, who, at all events, did not hold it at the pleasure of the governed, and whose supremacy men did not venture, perhaps did not desire, to contest, whatever precautions might be taken against its oppressive exercise. Their power was regarded as necessary, but also as highly dangerous; as a weapon which they would attempt to use against their subjects, no less than against external enemies. To prevent the weaker members of the community from being preyed upon by innumerable vultures, it was needful that there should be an animal of prey stronger than the rest, commissioned to keep them down. But as the king of the vultures would be no less bent upon preying on the flock than any of the minor harpies, it was indispensable to be in a perpetual attitude of defence against his beak and claws.
