About the book

The novel opens on a Kentucky farm, where the indebted Mr Shelby agrees to sell two enslaved people: Tom, a deeply religious and dignified man, and the young son of Eliza Harris. Rather than lose her child, Eliza flees north toward freedom, while Tom is carried down the Mississippi to be sold. The story follows both paths, the escape of Eliza and her husband George pursued by slave catchers, and Tom's life among new owners, including the kindly Augustine St. Clare and his saintly daughter Eva, and later the brutal planter Simon Legree.

Stowe set out to make readers feel the human cost of slavery, especially the destruction of families, and she appealed directly to Christian conscience and to mothers. Her style is emotional and openly persuasive, typical of sentimental fiction of the period. Modern readers often criticise its racial stereotypes, yet it remains essential for understanding how literature shaped public opinion on the eve of the Civil War.

Written in response to the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, the story was serialised in the antislavery paper The National Era before appearing as a book in 1852. It was enormously widely read in the United States and Britain, and was fiercely attacked in the South. Countless stage versions, often distorting its message, followed, and the name Uncle Tom later became an insult quite different from Stowe's intent.

Originally serialized in an abolitionist newspaper before its 1852 book publication.

About Harriet Beecher Stowe

Harriet Beecher was born in 1811 in Litchfield, Connecticut, one of the many children of the Calvinist preacher Lyman Beecher. She taught at her sister Catharine's school in Hartford, then moved to Cincinnati, across the river from slave-holding Kentucky, where she met people who had escaped slavery. She married Calvin Stowe, a professor of theology, and raised seven children. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 moved her to write. She later lived in Hartford and died there in 1896.

Uncle Tom's Cabin was serialised in an anti-slavery newspaper and published as a book in 1852. It follows the gentle, enslaved Tom and the escaping Eliza, exposing the cruelty of slavery. It became the best-selling novel of the nineteenth century and deeply influenced public opinion before the Civil War. Later readers have criticised its racial stereotypes, and the phrase Uncle Tom took on a negative meaning. She also wrote Dred and novels of New England life.

How it begins

The traveller in the south must often have remarked that peculiar air of refinement, that softness of voice and manner, which seems in many cases to be a particular gift to the quadroon and mulatto women. These natural graces in the quadroon are often united with beauty of the most dazzling kind, and in almost every case with a personal appearance prepossessing and agreeable. Eliza, such as we have described her, is not a fancy sketch, but taken from remembrance, as we saw her, years ago, in Kentucky. Safe under the protecting care of her mistress, Eliza had reached maturity without those temptations which make beauty so fatal an inheritance to a slave. She had been married to a bright and talented young mulatto man, who was a slave on a neighboring estate, and bore the name of George Harris.

This young man had been hired out by his master to work in a bagging factory, where his adroitness and ingenuity caused him to be considered the first hand in the place. He had invented a machine for the cleaning of the hemp, which, considering the education and circumstances of the inventor, displayed quite as much mechanical genius as Whitney’s cotton-gin.[1]

[2] A machine of this description was really the invention of a young colored man in Kentucky. [Mrs. Stowe’s note.]

He was possessed of a handsome person and pleasing manners, and was a general favorite in the factory. Nevertheless, as this young man was in the eye of the law not a man, but a thing, all these superior qualifications were subject to the control of a vulgar, narrow-minded, tyrannical master. This same gentleman, having heard of the fame of George’s invention, took a ride over to the factory, to see what this intelligent chattel had been about. He was received with great enthusiasm by the employer, who congratulated him on possessing so valuable a slave.

He was waited upon over the factory, shown the machinery by George, who, in high spirits, talked so fluently, held himself so erect, looked so handsome and manly, that his master began to feel an uneasy consciousness of inferiority. What business had his slave to be marching round the country, inventing machines, and holding up his head among gentlemen? He’d soon put a stop to it. He’d take him back, and put him to hoeing and digging, and “see if he’d step about so smart.” Accordingly, the manufacturer and all hands concerned were astounded when he suddenly demanded George’s wages, and announced his intention of taking him home.

“But, Mr. Harris,” remonstrated the manufacturer, “isn’t this rather sudden?”

“What if it is?—isn’t the man mine?”

“We would be willing, sir, to increase the rate of compensation.”
