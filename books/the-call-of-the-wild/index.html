About the book

Buck, a large and powerful dog, lives a comfortable life on Judge Miller's estate in California's Santa Clara Valley. But it is the 1890s, gold has been found in the Klondike, and strong dogs are in demand. Stolen by a gardener's helper and sold north, Buck is thrown into the harsh world of the Yukon, where he must learn the law of club and fang, pull sleds through snow and survive among rival dogs and masters of very different kinds.

Told largely from Buck's point of view, the short novel is an adventure story with deeper questions about civilisation, instinct and survival. Jack London writes with vivid physical detail about cold, hunger and endurance, and he shows how an animal shaped by comfort discovers older, wilder impulses within himself. It is gripping for young readers and thought-provoking for adults, especially in its depiction of how humans treat the animals they depend on.

London had travelled to the Klondike himself during the gold rush of 1897, and that experience fed directly into the book. The Call of the Wild was serialised in The Saturday Evening Post and published as a book in 1903, and it brought him wide fame. He later wrote White Fang (1906) as a kind of companion piece. The story has been adapted many times for film, television and animation.

First serialized in The Saturday Evening Post in 1903.

The Call of the Wild follows Buck, a large, comfortable dog living on a California estate, who is stolen from his home and sold into the brutal world of the Klondike Gold Rush, where he is shipped north to become a sled dog. Thrust abruptly from domestic comfort into a harsh, often violent world governed by "the law of club and fang," Buck quickly learns that survival requires abandoning civilized restraint: he watches the dominant sled dog Spitz enforce order through violence, endures brutal treatment from careless and cruel owners, and steals food to survive, feeling ancestral instincts long dormant beneath his domesticated nature beginning to stir. After a grueling rivalry, Buck kills Spitz in a fight for pack leadership and proves himself an exceptional lead dog under a competent, careful team of mail carriers, before being sold through a succession of owners of varying competence and cruelty, culminating in the incompetent, needlessly cruel Hal, Charles, and Mercedes, who nearly work the team to death through their ignorance of the trail. Buck and the surviving dogs are rescued just before near-certain death by John Thornton, an experienced, humane outdoorsman who nurses Buck back to health and with whom Buck forms the deepest bond of loyalty and love in his life, saving Thornton's life more than once and winning a substantial wager by single-handedly pulling an enormously overloaded sled. Even amid this profound devotion, Buck feels an increasingly powerful pull toward the wild, drawn by the howling of wolves in the surrounding forest and by dreams of a primitive, ancestral past, making brief forays into the wilderness to hunt before returning each time to Thornton's camp. When Thornton and his companions are killed by raiding Yeehat natives while Buck is away hunting, the last tether binding Buck to human civilization is severed. Buck kills several of the Yeehats in a display of terrifying, primal power, and, finally free of any human claim upon him, answers the call he has felt building throughout the novel, joining a wolf pack and disappearing fully into the wild, eventually becoming the subject of Yeehat legend as a fearsome Ghost Dog running at the head of the wolves.

About Jack London

Jack London was born in San Francisco in 1876 and grew up in poverty around Oakland. As a teenager he worked in factories, raided oyster beds, sailed to the Pacific on a sealing schooner and tramped across the country, becoming a committed socialist. After a short time at the University of California, he joined the Klondike Gold Rush in 1897. He became one of the best-paid writers of his day and died at his California ranch in 1916, aged forty.

London's fame began with stories of the Yukon. The Call of the Wild, published in 1903, follows Buck, a stolen dog who answers the call of his wild ancestry; White Fang reverses the journey. He also wrote The Sea-Wolf, the dystopian The Iron Heel, the semi-autobiographical Martin Eden, and stories such as To Build a Fire. His vigorous prose about survival, nature and social struggle made him one of the most widely read and translated American authors.

How it begins

Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that trouble was brewing, not alone for himself, but for every tide-water dog, strong of muscle and with warm, long hair, from Puget Sound to San Diego. Because men, groping in the Arctic darkness, had found a yellow metal, and because steamship and transportation companies were booming the find, thousands of men were rushing into the Northland. These men wanted dogs, and the dogs they wanted were heavy dogs, with strong muscles by which to toil, and furry coats to protect them from the frost.

Buck lived at a big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley. Judge Miller’s place, it was called. It stood back from the road, half hidden among the trees, through which glimpses could be caught of the wide cool veranda that ran around its four sides. The house was approached by gravelled driveways which wound about through wide-spreading lawns and under the interlacing boughs of tall poplars. At the rear things were on even a more spacious scale than at the front. There were great stables, where a dozen grooms and boys held forth, rows of vine-clad servants’ cottages, an endless and orderly array of outhouses, long grape arbors, green pastures, orchards, and berry patches. Then there was the pumping plant for the artesian well, and the big cement tank where Judge Miller’s boys took their morning plunge and kept cool in the hot afternoon.

And over this great demesne Buck ruled. Here he was born, and here he had lived the four years of his life. It was true, there were other dogs. There could not but be other dogs on so vast a place, but they did not count. They came and went, resided in the populous kennels, or lived obscurely in the recesses of the house after the fashion of Toots, the Japanese pug, or Ysabel, the Mexican hairless,—strange creatures that rarely put nose out of doors or set foot to ground. On the other hand, there were the fox terriers, a score of them at least, who yelped fearful promises at Toots and Ysabel looking out of the windows at them and protected by a legion of housemaids armed with brooms and mops.

But Buck was neither house-dog nor kennel-dog. The whole realm was his. He plunged into the swimming tank or went hunting with the Judge’s sons; he escorted Mollie and Alice, the Judge’s daughters, on long twilight or early morning rambles; on wintry nights he lay at the Judge’s feet before the roaring library fire; he carried the Judge’s grandsons on his back, or rolled them in the grass, and guarded their footsteps through wild adventures down to the fountain in the stable yard, and even beyond, where the paddocks were, and the berry patches. Among the terriers he stalked imperiously, and Toots and Ysabel he utterly ignored, for he was king,—king over all creeping, crawling, flying things of Judge Miller’s place, humans included.
