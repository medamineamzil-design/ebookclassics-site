About the book

White Fang is born in a cave in the frozen Yukon wilderness, the only survivor of a litter fathered by a wolf and born to Kiche, a she-wolf with dog's blood in her veins. His first lessons are those of the wild: eat or be eaten. When he and his mother wander into a Native American camp, he passes into the hands of Grey Beaver and learns a new law, the power of the gods who make fire, but also the hostility of the other dogs, who turn him into a lonely and ferocious fighter.

The novel follows White Fang from master to master: the stern but fair Grey Beaver, the cruel Beauty Smith who makes him a fighting dog for money, and finally Weedon Scott, a mining engineer who rescues him and slowly wins his trust with patience and kindness. Jack London tells the story largely from the animal's point of view, with vivid, physical writing about cold, hunger and combat, and a clear belief that character is shaped by environment as much as by instinct.

London published White Fang in 1906, first as a serial in The Outing Magazine, as a deliberate companion to The Call of the Wild (1903): where Buck goes from civilisation to the wild, White Fang travels the opposite road, from the wild to a home in California. Drawing on London's own time in the Klondike gold rush, it became one of his most popular books and has been adapted several times for film and animation.

About Jack London

Jack London was born in San Francisco in 1876 and grew up in poverty around Oakland. As a teenager he worked in factories, raided oyster beds, sailed to the Pacific on a sealing schooner and tramped across the country, becoming a committed socialist. After a short time at the University of California, he joined the Klondike Gold Rush in 1897. He became one of the best-paid writers of his day and died at his California ranch in 1916, aged forty.

London's fame began with stories of the Yukon. The Call of the Wild, published in 1903, follows Buck, a stolen dog who answers the call of his wild ancestry; White Fang reverses the journey. He also wrote The Sea-Wolf, the dystopian The Iron Heel, the semi-autobiographical Martin Eden, and stories such as To Build a Fire. His vigorous prose about survival, nature and social struggle made him one of the most widely read and translated American authors.

How it begins

Dark spruce forest frowned on either side the frozen waterway. The trees had been stripped by a recent wind of their white covering of frost, and they seemed to lean towards each other, black and ominous, in the fading light. A vast silence reigned over the land. The land itself was a desolation, lifeless, without movement, so lone and cold that the spirit of it was not even that of sadness. There was a hint in it of laughter, but of a laughter more terrible than any sadness—a laughter that was mirthless as the smile of the sphinx, a laughter cold as the frost and partaking of the grimness of infallibility. It was the masterful and incommunicable wisdom of eternity laughing at the futility of life and the effort of life. It was the Wild, the savage, frozen-hearted Northland Wild.

But there was life, abroad in the land and defiant. Down the frozen waterway toiled a string of wolfish dogs. Their bristly fur was rimed with frost. Their breath froze in the air as it left their mouths, spouting forth in spumes of vapour that settled upon the hair of their bodies and formed into crystals of frost. Leather harness was on the dogs, and leather traces attached them to a sled which dragged along behind. The sled was without runners. It was made of stout birch-bark, and its full surface rested on the snow. The front end of the sled was turned up, like a scroll, in order to force down and under the bore of soft snow that surged like a wave before it. On the sled, securely lashed, was a long and narrow oblong box. There were other things on the sled—blankets, an axe, and a coffee-pot and frying-pan; but prominent, occupying most of the space, was the long and narrow oblong box.

In advance of the dogs, on wide snowshoes, toiled a man. At the rear of the sled toiled a second man. On the sled, in the box, lay a third man whose toil was over,—a man whom the Wild had conquered and beaten down until he would never move nor struggle again. It is not the way of the Wild to like movement. Life is an offence to it, for life is movement; and the Wild aims always to destroy movement. It freezes the water to prevent it running to the sea; it drives the sap out of the trees till they are frozen to their mighty hearts; and most ferociously and terribly of all does the Wild harry and crush into submission man—man who is the most restless of life, ever in revolt against the dictum that all movement must in the end come to the cessation of movement.

But at front and rear, unawed and indomitable, toiled the two men who were not yet dead. Their bodies were covered with fur and soft-tanned leather. Eyelashes and cheeks and lips were so coated with the crystals from their frozen breath that their faces were not discernible. This gave them the seeming of ghostly masques, undertakers in a spectral world at the funeral of some ghost. But under it all they were men, penetrating the land of desolation and mockery and silence, puny adventurers bent on colossal adventure, pitting themselves against the might of a world as remote and alien and pulseless as the abysses of space.
