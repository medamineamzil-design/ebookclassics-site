About the book

The Great Gatsby is narrated by Nick Carraway, a young Midwesterner who moves to Long Island in the summer of 1922 to work in the bond business in New York. His neighbour in West Egg is Jay Gatsby, a mysterious millionaire whose lavish parties draw crowds of strangers. Across the bay in fashionable East Egg live Nick's cousin Daisy and her wealthy, overbearing husband Tom Buchanan. As Nick is drawn into Gatsby's world, he discovers that the parties, the mansion and the fortune all serve a single, carefully hidden purpose rooted in Gatsby's past.

The novel explores the American Dream and its corruption, the gulf between old money and new, and the impossibility of recapturing the past. Fitzgerald's prose is compact and lyrical, full of memorable images such as the green light at the end of Daisy's dock and the brooding billboard eyes over a desolate valley of ashes. Short enough to read in an afternoon, it rewards rereading.

Published by Scribner's in April 1925, the book received mixed reviews and modest attention during Fitzgerald's lifetime, and he died believing himself largely forgotten. Its reputation grew dramatically after the Second World War, and it became a fixture of school reading in the United States. It has been adapted for film, stage, opera and graphic novels, and entered the American public domain in 2021.

The Great Gatsby is narrated by Nick Carraway, a Yale-educated Midwesterner who moves to Long Island in the summer of 1922 to work in bonds and rents a modest house next to the mansion of the mysterious, fabulously wealthy Jay Gatsby, famous for throwing enormous, extravagant parties that he himself rarely appears to enjoy. Nick reconnects with his cousin Daisy Buchanan, married to the brutish, unfaithful Tom Buchanan across the bay, and gradually learns that Gatsby's entire, meticulously constructed life, the mansion, the parties, the invented persona of effortless old wealth, has been built for one purpose: to win back Daisy, whom he loved and lost as a poor young army officer five years earlier, before amassing his fortune, implied to be through bootlegging and other illicit dealings, specifically in hopes of becoming someone she could marry. Nick arranges a reunion, and Gatsby and Daisy begin a secret affair, Gatsby convinced that if he simply provides enough wealth and spectacle he can erase the intervening years entirely and recreate their past exactly as it was. Tom, threatened both by Gatsby's growing claim on Daisy and by his own guilt over an ongoing affair with Myrtle Wilson, the wife of a struggling garage owner, confronts Gatsby directly during a tense gathering at the Plaza Hotel, exposing the criminal source of his wealth and forcing Daisy to choose between the two men. Daisy, unable to fully commit to Gatsby's demand that she declare she never loved Tom, wavers, and the group drives back separately; Daisy, driving Gatsby's car with Gatsby beside her, strikes and kills Myrtle Wilson, who had run into the road believing the car was Tom's. Gatsby, still devoted, resolves to take the blame for Daisy, but Tom tells Myrtle's grief-stricken, distraught husband George that the car belongs to Gatsby, allowing George to wrongly conclude Gatsby was both Myrtle's killer and her lover. George shoots Gatsby dead in his own pool before killing himself. In the novel's bleak coda, almost no one attends Gatsby's funeral, Daisy and Tom retreat without consequence into their wealth, and Nick, disillusioned by the moral emptiness he has witnessed, returns to the Midwest, reflecting on Gatsby's tragic, doomed faith in the American capacity to repeat and perfect the past.

About F. Scott Fitzgerald

Francis Scott Key Fitzgerald was born in 1896 in St Paul, Minnesota. He studied at Princeton but left without a degree and joined the army in 1917. While stationed in Alabama he met Zelda Sayre, and after his first novel, This Side of Paradise, succeeded in 1920, they married and became celebrities. Later years brought financial trouble, heavy drinking and Zelda's mental illness. Fitzgerald moved to Hollywood to write screenplays, and he died there of a heart attack in 1940, aged forty-four.

Fitzgerald's story collection Tales of the Jazz Age helped give the 1920s their name. The Great Gatsby, published in 1925, tells of the mysterious millionaire Jay Gatsby and his longing for Daisy Buchanan, as seen by the narrator Nick Carraway. Its lyrical prose and portrait of wealth, desire and disillusion made it a defining American novel. He also wrote Tender Is the Night, the unfinished The Last Tycoon and many short stories, including Babylon Revisited.

How it begins

He didn’t say any more, but we’ve always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that. In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores. The abnormal mind is quick to detect and attach itself to this quality when it appears in a normal person, and so it came about that in college I was unjustly accused of being a politician, because I was privy to the secret griefs of wild, unknown men. Most of the confidences were unsought—frequently I have feigned sleep, preoccupation, or a hostile levity when I realized by some unmistakable sign that an intimate revelation was quivering on the horizon; for the intimate revelations of young men, or at least the terms in which they express them, are usually plagiaristic and marred by obvious suppressions. Reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope. I am still a little afraid of missing something if I forget that, as my father snobbishly suggested, and I snobbishly repeat, a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out unequally at birth.

And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit. Conduct may be founded on the hard rock or the wet marshes, but after a certain point I don’t care what it’s founded on. When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever; I wanted no more riotous excursions with privileged glimpses into the human heart. Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, as if he were related to one of those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten thousand miles away. This responsiveness had nothing to do with that flabby impressionability which is dignified under the name of the “creative temperament”—it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again. No—Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men.
