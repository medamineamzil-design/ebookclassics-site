About the book

In seventeenth-century Puritan Boston, a young woman named Hester Prynne stands on the scaffold holding her infant daughter, Pearl, condemned to wear a scarlet letter A on her gown as a mark of adultery. She refuses to name the child's father. Watching from the crowd is a stranger, in fact her long-absent husband, who takes the name Roger Chillingworth and quietly sets out to discover the man who wronged him. The novel follows Hester, Pearl and the people around them over the following years as secrets work on everyone involved.

Hawthorne explores sin, guilt, shame, hypocrisy and the harshness of a community that punishes in public while hiding its own failings. Hester's quiet strength and dignity make her one of the great heroines of American literature, and the novel's rich symbolism, centred on the letter itself, invites many readings. Its brooding, psychological style still feels intense and modern.

The book was published in Boston in 1850 and made Hawthorne's reputation almost overnight. It opens with a long autobiographical sketch, The Custom-House, describing his time working in Salem, where he claims to have found the letter. Hawthorne was himself descended from a judge in the Salem witch trials, and his novel is often read as a reckoning with New England's Puritan past. It has been adapted for film, television, opera and the stage.

About Nathaniel Hawthorne

Nathaniel Hawthorne was born in 1804 in Salem, Massachusetts. One of his Puritan ancestors had been a judge at the Salem witch trials, a legacy that troubled him. His father, a sea captain, died when Nathaniel was a child. At Bowdoin College his classmates included the poet Longfellow and the future president Franklin Pierce. He spent years writing in seclusion, briefly joined the utopian community of Brook Farm, and married Sophia Peabody. He worked at the Salem Custom House and as US consul in Liverpool, and died in 1864.

His stories include Young Goodman Brown and Rappaccini's Daughter. The Scarlet Letter, published in 1850, tells of Hester Prynne, forced to wear a scarlet A for adultery in Puritan Boston, and of the hidden guilt of her lover, the minister Dimmesdale. It was followed by The House of the Seven Gables. Hawthorne explored sin, guilt and the weight of the past, and he influenced Herman Melville, who dedicated Moby-Dick to him.

How it begins

Much to the author’s surprise, and (if he may say so without additional offence) considerably to his amusement, he finds that his sketch of official life, introductory to THE SCARLET LETTER, has created an unprecedented excitement in the respectable community immediately around him. It could hardly have been more violent, indeed, had he burned down the Custom-House, and quenched its last smoking ember in the blood of a certain venerable personage, against whom he is supposed to cherish a peculiar malevolence. As the public disapprobation would weigh very heavily on him, were he conscious of deserving it, the author begs leave to say, that he has carefully read over the introductory pages, with a purpose to alter or expunge whatever might be found amiss, and to make the best reparation in his power for the atrocities of which he has been adjudged guilty. But it appears to him, that the only remarkable features of the sketch are its frank and genuine good-humor, and the general accuracy with which he has conveyed his sincere impressions of the characters therein described. As to enmity, or ill-feeling of any kind, personal or political, he utterly disclaims such motives. The sketch might, perhaps, have been wholly omitted, without loss to the public, or detriment to the book; but, having undertaken to write it, he conceives that it could not have been done in a better or a kindlier spirit, nor, so far as his abilities availed, with a livelier effect of truth.

The author is constrained, therefore, to republish his introductory sketch without the change of a word.

SALEM, March 30, 1850.

The Custom-house — Introductory to “the Scarlet Letter.”

It is a little remarkable, that—though disinclined to talk overmuch of myself and my affairs at the fireside, and to my personal friends—an autobiographical impulse should twice in my life have taken possession of me, in addressing the public. The first time was three or four years since, when I favored the reader—inexcusably, and for no earthly reason, that either the indulgent reader or the intrusive author could imagine—with a description of my way of life in the deep quietude of an Old Manse. And now—because, beyond my deserts, I was happy enough to find a listener or two on the former occasion—I again seize the public by the button, and talk of my three years’ experience in a Custom-House. The example of the famous “P. P., Clerk of this Parish,” was never more faithfully followed. The truth seems to be, however, that, when he casts his leaves forth upon the wind, the author addresses, not the many who will fling aside his volume, or never take it up, but the few who will understand him, better than most of his schoolmates or lifemates.
