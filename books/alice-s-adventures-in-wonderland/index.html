About the book

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland begins with Alice, a bored little girl sitting by a riverbank, following a White Rabbit in a waistcoat who frets that he is late and disappears down a rabbit hole. Alice follows and falls for what feels like an impossibly long time, landing in a hall of locked doors where she discovers a bottle labeled "Drink Me" and a cake labeled "Eat Me," both of which alter her size dramatically and unpredictably, a running device Carroll uses throughout to disorient both Alice and the reader. From there the narrative proceeds as a loosely connected sequence of encounters rather than a conventional plot: Alice swims through a pool of her own tears alongside a Mouse and assorted animals; attends a chaotic Caucus Race with no clear rules or winner; meets a hookah-smoking Caterpillar who questions her identity and teaches her to control her size by nibbling different sides of a mushroom; visits the Duchess's chaotic kitchen, where a baby turns into a pig; and joins the Mad Hatter's perpetual tea party alongside the March Hare and the sleepy Dormouse, an event stuck eternally at six o'clock because the Hatter has quarreled with Time itself. Alice eventually reaches the garden of the Queen of Hearts, a domain of playing-card gardeners, a surreal croquet match played with flamingos as mallets and hedgehogs as balls, and a monarch whose default response to any inconvenience is "Off with their head!" The book culminates in the trial of the Knave of Hearts, accused of stealing tarts, a proceeding of nonsensical legal logic in which Alice, having grown to her full size and lost her patience with the entire company, finally declares them "nothing but a pack of cards," at which point the whole world collapses and she wakes on the riverbank beside her sister, the fantastical kingdom revealed as a dream.

About Lewis Carroll

English writer and mathematician (1832-1898), pen name of Charles Dodgson, best known for the fantasy novel Alice's Adventures in Wonderland.

How it begins

Either the well was very deep, or she fell very slowly, for she had plenty of time as she went down to look about her and to wonder what was going to happen next. First, she tried to look down and make out what she was coming to, but it was too dark to see anything; then she looked at the sides of the well, and noticed that they were filled with cupboards and book-shelves; here and there she saw maps and pictures hung upon pegs. She took down a jar from one of the shelves as she passed; it was labelled “ORANGE MARMALADE”, but to her great disappointment it was empty: she did not like to drop the jar for fear of killing somebody underneath, so managed to put it into one of the cupboards as she fell past it.
