About the book

Told in epistolary form: diary entries, letters, and telegrams.

Dracula is told entirely through letters, diary entries, telegrams, and newspaper clippings, opening with the young English solicitor Jonathan Harker traveling to Transylvania to finalize a property transaction for the mysterious Count Dracula. Harker gradually realizes he is a prisoner in the count's remote castle, observes Dracula's inhuman abilities, scaling walls like a lizard, commanding wolves, and escapes only after the count departs for England aboard a ship, the Demeter, whose crew are all found dead or missing upon arrival, the vessel steered into port by a corpse lashed to the helm. In England, Dracula begins preying upon Lucy Westenra, a friend of Harker's fiancée Mina, who grows mysteriously ill despite multiple blood transfusions arranged by her suitor Dr. Seward and the eccentric Dutch specialist Professor Abraham Van Helsing, who alone recognizes the vampiric cause of her decline. Lucy dies and is buried, but soon after is discovered to be rising from her grave at night to prey on local children as one of the undead; Van Helsing, Seward, Lucy's fiancé Arthur Holmwood, and the American Quincey Morris hunt her down, and Arthur, in an agonizing act of mercy, drives a stake through her heart to release her soul and restore her to peaceful death. Meanwhile Harker returns and marries Mina, and the group, now including the Harkers, unites against Dracula, who has relocated to London and begun secretly feeding on Mina herself, forcing her to drink his blood in a horrifying scene that spiritually binds her to him and marks her for eventual transformation unless he is destroyed. Using Mina's resulting telepathic connection to the count, alongside Van Helsing's expertise in vampire lore and an array of religious and folkloric countermeasures, garlic, crucifixes, communion wafers, the group tracks Dracula as he flees back toward his Transylvanian castle. In a final pursuit across the Carpathian countryside, they intercept Dracula's coffin at sunset, and Jonathan Harker and Quincey Morris kill the count, Harker slitting his throat and Morris stabbing him through the heart, though Morris dies of wounds sustained in the fight. Dracula crumbles to dust, Mina is freed of the curse, and an epilogue reveals the Harkers married with a son partly named after the fallen Quincey.

About Bram Stoker

Irish author (1847–1912), best known for the Gothic horror novel Dracula (1897), a foundational work of vampire fiction.

How it begins

3 May. Bistritz.--Left Munich at 8:35 P. M., on 1st May, arriving at Vienna early next morning; should have arrived at 6:46, but train was an hour late. Buda-Pesth seems a wonderful place, from the glimpse which I got of it from the train and the little I could walk through the streets. I feared to go very far from the station, as we had arrived late and would start as near the correct time as possible. The impression I had was that we were leaving the West and entering the East; the most western of splendid bridges over the Danube, which is here of noble width and depth, took us among the traditions of Turkish rule.

We left in pretty good time, and came after nightfall to Klausenburgh. Here I stopped for the night at the Hotel Royale. I had for dinner, or rather supper, a chicken done up some way with red pepper, which was very good but thirsty. (Mem., get recipe for Mina.) I asked the waiter, and he said it was called “paprika hendl,” and that, as it was a national dish, I should be able to get it anywhere along the Carpathians. I found my smattering of German very useful here; indeed, I don’t know how I should be able to get on without it.
