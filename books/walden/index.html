About the book

On 4 July 1845, Henry David Thoreau moved into a small cabin he had built himself beside Walden Pond, near Concord, Massachusetts, on land owned by his friend Ralph Waldo Emerson. He lived there for about two years, and Walden is his account of the experiment, compressed into the cycle of a single year. He begins with a detailed look at what he actually needed in food, shelter and clothing, then turns to reading, solitude, visitors, his bean field, the animals of the woods and the changing life of the pond through the seasons.

The book is part nature writing, part philosophy and part sharp social criticism. Thoreau questions the busy, debt-driven lives of his neighbours and asks what it means to live deliberately and simply. His prose moves from precise observation to bold, playful aphorism, and readers today often find his reflections on consumption, work, technology and attention surprisingly relevant.

Walden was published by Ticknor and Fields in Boston in 1854 after several years of revision, and it sold modestly at first. Its reputation grew steadily after Thoreau's death in 1862. During his stay at the pond he was briefly jailed for refusing to pay a poll tax, an episode behind his essay on civil disobedience. Walden is now seen as a founding text of American environmental thought and continues to inspire writers, naturalists and advocates of simple living.

About Henry David Thoreau

Henry David Thoreau was born in 1817 in Concord, Massachusetts, where his family made pencils. He graduated from Harvard in 1837, taught school with his brother, and became a friend and protégé of Ralph Waldo Emerson, joining the Transcendentalist circle. From July 1845 to September 1847 he lived in a small cabin he built at Walden Pond, on land owned by Emerson. In 1846 he spent a night in jail for refusing to pay a poll tax in protest against slavery. He worked as a surveyor, opposed slavery, and died in Concord of tuberculosis in 1862.

Thoreau's essay Resistance to Civil Government, later known as Civil Disobedience, argued that individuals should refuse to cooperate with unjust laws. Walden, published in 1854, reflects on his life in the woods, on simplicity, self-reliance and close observation of nature. His ideas on peaceful resistance influenced Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr., and his writing on nature inspired the environmental movement.

How it begins

When I wrote the following pages, or rather the bulk of them, I lived alone, in the woods, a mile from any neighbor, in a house which I had built myself, on the shore of Walden Pond, in Concord, Massachusetts, and earned my living by the labor of my hands only. I lived there two years and two months. At present I am a sojourner in civilized life again.

I should not obtrude my affairs so much on the notice of my readers if very particular inquiries had not been made by my townsmen concerning my mode of life, which some would call impertinent, though they do not appear to me at all impertinent, but, considering the circumstances, very natural and pertinent. Some have asked what I got to eat; if I did not feel lonesome; if I was not afraid; and the like. Others have been curious to learn what portion of my income I devoted to charitable purposes; and some, who have large families, how many poor children I maintained. I will therefore ask those of my readers who feel no particular interest in me to pardon me if I undertake to answer some of these questions in this book. In most books, the I, or first person, is omitted; in this it will be retained; that, in respect to egotism, is the main difference. We commonly do not remember that it is, after all, always the first person that is speaking. I should not talk so much about myself if there were anybody else whom I knew as well. Unfortunately, I am confined to this theme by the narrowness of my experience. Moreover, I, on my side, require of every writer, first or last, a simple and sincere account of his own life, and not merely what he has heard of other men’s lives; some such account as he would send to his kindred from a distant land; for if he has lived sincerely, it must have been in a distant land to me. Perhaps these pages are more particularly addressed to poor students.…

I have travelled a good deal in Concord; and everywhere, in shops, and offices, and fields, the inhabitants have appeared to me to be doing penance in a thousand remarkable ways.
