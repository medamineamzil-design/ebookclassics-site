About the book

In a small Russian town, the dissolute and greedy landowner Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov lives in open conflict with his sons. Dmitri, the eldest, is passionate and reckless, and quarrels with his father over money and over the same woman, Grushenka. Ivan is a brilliant intellectual tormented by the question of how a good God can allow suffering. Alyosha, the youngest, is a gentle novice at the local monastery under the wise Elder Zosima. Also in the household is the servant Smerdyakov, rumoured to be another son. As tensions rise, a violent crime throws the family into crisis and sets in motion an investigation and trial.

The novel is at once a family drama, a murder mystery and a profound debate about faith, doubt, freedom and moral responsibility. Its most famous chapter, The Grand Inquisitor, presents Ivan's parable of Christ returning during the Spanish Inquisition. Dostoevsky gives powerful voice to belief and unbelief alike, and his intense, feverish characters remain unforgettable.

The Brothers Karamazov was serialised in the journal The Russian Messenger in 1879 and 1880, and Dostoevsky died only a few months after its completion. It is widely regarded as his greatest work and has influenced novelists, philosophers, theologians and psychologists. It has been adapted for film, television and the stage in Russia and elsewhere.

About Fyodor Dostoevsky

Fyodor Dostoevsky was born in Moscow in 1821, the son of a doctor at a hospital for the poor. He trained as a military engineer in St Petersburg but left the service to write, and his first novel, Poor Folk, was warmly received in 1846. In 1849 he was arrested for joining a radical discussion circle, subjected to a mock execution, and sentenced to four years of hard labour in Siberia. He struggled with epilepsy, gambling and debt. He died in St Petersburg in 1881.

Dostoevsky drew on his prison years in The House of the Dead, and Notes from Underground introduced a bitter, self-contradicting narrator. His great novels followed: Crime and Punishment, about a poor student who commits murder to test a theory; The Idiot; Demons; and The Brothers Karamazov, which wrestles with faith, doubt and responsibility. His psychological depth influenced existentialist philosophers and countless novelists, and his books remain among the most powerful explorations of the human conscience.

How it begins

Alexey Fyodorovitch Karamazov was the third son of Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov, a land owner well known in our district in his own day, and still remembered among us owing to his gloomy and tragic death, which happened thirteen years ago, and which I shall describe in its proper place. For the present I will only say that this “landowner”—for so we used to call him, although he hardly spent a day of his life on his own estate—was a strange type, yet one pretty frequently to be met with, a type abject and vicious and at the same time senseless. But he was one of those senseless persons who are very well capable of looking after their worldly affairs, and, apparently, after nothing else. Fyodor Pavlovitch, for instance, began with next to nothing; his estate was of the smallest; he ran to dine at other men’s tables, and fastened on them as a toady, yet at his death it appeared that he had a hundred thousand roubles in hard cash. At the same time, he was all his life one of the most senseless, fantastical fellows in the whole district. I repeat, it was not stupidity—the majority of these fantastical fellows are shrewd and intelligent enough—but just senselessness, and a peculiar national form of it.

He was married twice, and had three sons, the eldest, Dmitri, by his first wife, and two, Ivan and Alexey, by his second. Fyodor Pavlovitch’s first wife, Adelaïda Ivanovna, belonged to a fairly rich and distinguished noble family, also landowners in our district, the Miüsovs. How it came to pass that an heiress, who was also a beauty, and moreover one of those vigorous, intelligent girls, so common in this generation, but sometimes also to be found in the last, could have married such a worthless, puny weakling, as we all called him, I won’t attempt to explain. I knew a young lady of the last “romantic” generation who after some years of an enigmatic passion for a gentleman, whom she might quite easily have married at any moment, invented insuperable obstacles to their union, and ended by throwing herself one stormy night into a rather deep and rapid river from a high bank, almost a precipice, and so perished, entirely to satisfy her own caprice, and to be like Shakespeare’s Ophelia. Indeed, if this precipice, a chosen and favorite spot of hers, had been less picturesque, if there had been a prosaic flat bank in its place, most likely the suicide would never have taken place. This is a fact, and probably there have been not a few similar instances in the last two or three generations.
