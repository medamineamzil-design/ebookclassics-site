About the book

The War of the Worlds is narrated by an unnamed writer living in Woking, England, who describes the sudden arrival of cylindrical Martian spacecraft that crash-land on Horsell Common, initially drawing curious crowds who assume the visitors, if hostile at all, could not possibly outmatch human weaponry. This assumption is shattered almost immediately when the Martians emerge, tentacled, slug-like creatures piloting massive mechanical fighting-machines armed with a devastating Heat-Ray, and incinerate the gathered crowd along with the delegation sent to greet them peacefully. As additional cylinders land across the English countryside, the Martians rapidly overwhelm the British military's conventional artillery and cavalry, and the narrator, separated from his wife during the ensuing panic, embarks on a harrowing journey through a collapsing society, encountering a terrified, fatalistic curate who eventually descends into dangerous madness while the two are trapped together in a ruined house near a Martian encampment, and an artilleryman who dreams grandiosely of humanity rebuilding a resistance movement in underground sewers, a plan that gradually reveals itself as mostly idle fantasy and self-justifying laziness. Meanwhile the Martians deploy a second, even more terrifying weapon, the Black Smoke, a poisonous gas that annihilates everything in its path, and begin harvesting captured humans for blood, revealed gradually as their actual means of nourishment, a detail that recasts the entire invasion as humanity abruptly finding itself in the position of prey rather than the presumed pinnacle of creation. London descends into chaotic, mass evacuation and social collapse as the Martians advance on the capital virtually unopposed by any human weapon, the narrator witnessing the wholesale reordering of civilization's assumed hierarchies within days. Just as human civilization appears on the verge of total defeat, the narrator discovers, wandering through an eerily silent, Martian-dominated London, that the war machines have fallen silent and their operators are dead, killed not by any human weapon but by ordinary earthly bacteria, pathogens against which the Martians, despite their vastly superior technology, had no evolved immunity. Humanity is left devastated but alive, and the narrator is eventually reunited with his wife, the novel closing with a sober reflection on humanity's diminished sense of its own security and centrality in the universe.

About H. G. Wells

English writer (1866-1946), a pioneer of science fiction known for The War of the Worlds, The Time Machine, and The Invisible Man.

How it begins

No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century that this world was being watched keenly and closely by intelligences greater than man’s and yet as mortal as his own; that as men busied themselves about their various concerns they were scrutinised and studied, perhaps almost as narrowly as a man with a microscope might scrutinise the transient creatures that swarm and multiply in a drop of water. With infinite complacency men went to and fro over this globe about their little affairs, serene in their assurance of their empire over matter. It is possible that the infusoria under the microscope do the same. No one gave a thought to the older worlds of space as sources of human danger, or thought of them only to dismiss the idea of life upon them as impossible or improbable. It is curious to recall some of the mental habits of those departed days. At most terrestrial men fancied there might be other men upon Mars, perhaps inferior to themselves and ready to welcome a missionary enterprise.
