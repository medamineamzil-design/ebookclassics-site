About the book

Commissioned by the Communist League and written jointly by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The Communist Manifesto was first published in London in February 1848, just weeks before a wave of revolutions swept across Europe, and it opens with one of the most famous sentences in political writing: "A spectre is haunting Europe — the spectre of communism." The pamphlet's first and most influential section lays out a theory of history in which all previously existing society is described as the history of class struggles — between freemen and slaves, patricians and plebeians, lords and serfs — and argues that the modern age has simplified this ancient conflict into two starkly opposed camps: the bourgeoisie, who own the factories, land, and capital that constitute the means of production, and the proletariat, the propertyless industrial working class who survive only by selling their labor for wages. Marx and Engels trace how the rising bourgeoisie violently overturned the feudal order, sweeping away hereditary privilege and static tradition, only to replace it with a new and in some ways starker form of domination, one stripped of sentiment and reduced, in the pamphlet's own phrase, to "naked self-interest" and "callous cash payment." The second section directly answers accusations leveled against communists by their opponents — that they wish to abolish private property, the family, and national identity — reframing these charges as, in the authors' view, accurate descriptions of what capitalism has already done to the working class, who own no meaningful property, whose family lives are disrupted by factory labor, and who possess no real stake in any nation. A short third section surveys and dismisses rival strands of socialism circulating in Europe at the time as either reactionary, conservative, or merely utopian, contrasting them with the authors' own claim to scientific analysis. The pamphlet closes with a brief statement of immediate political demands — among them a progressive income tax, free public education, and the abolition of child factory labor — and ends with the rallying cry that would become its most quoted line: "Working men of all countries, unite!"

About Karl Marx & Friedrich Engels

German philosophers and economists (Marx, 1818-1883; Engels, 1820-1895), co-authors of The Communist Manifesto and founders of Marxist theory.

How it begins

II. It is high time that Communists should openly, in the face of the whole world, publish their views, their aims, their tendencies, and meet this nursery tale of the Spectre of Communism with a Manifesto of the party itself.

To this end, Communists of various nationalities have assembled in London, and sketched the following Manifesto, to be published in the English, French, German, Italian, Flemish and Danish languages.

I. BOURGEOIS AND PROLETARIANS

The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles.

Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, in a word, oppressor and oppressed, stood in constant opposition to one another, carried on an uninterrupted, now hidden, now open fight, a fight that each time ended, either in a revolutionary re-constitution of society at large, or in the common ruin of the contending classes.
